The first full console game in the Compilation of Final Fantasy VII has one of
the worst reputations in the entire franchise. Its release is used as ammunition
against the Compilation. Is it as bad as everyone says?

No. I don't think so. It's pretty bad, but it's far from the bottom of the
ranking. It has flaws (plenty of them), but it also shines in a few places. This
community has a strong tendency to propagate memes about a game, giving it a
reputation that isn't actually earned. It's no coincidence that these memes are
always negative. People tend to be suspicious about praise, but not about hate.
Probably because it's easier to _not_ play a game than it is to play it, and
believing praise means missing out on something until you play it. This is the
worst victim of that phenomenon.

Now, I'm not defending this game as somehow one of the greatest masterpieces
that is simply misunderstood or anything. I just think it lies solidly in the
middle of the pack. I hope, through this review, I can accurately describe to
you why I feel that way.

# The Review

I was shocked by how good my first impressions were. It's likely that this was
influenced by my low expectations, but still. The first handful of levels are
actually extremely solid, have interesting level design, cool boss fights, and
so on. I have no complaints about the opening hours of this game. Unfortunately,
that impression didn't last, and it became a mixed bag pretty quickly. By the
end, it felt like a jumble of random, repetitive things thrown together just to
get the project done. Fortunately, even then a few good things stood out, but
not enough to save the game.

## Gameplay

This is a third person shooter with light RPG elements. You can definitely tell
that this was the first shooter put together by this team, but I think they
pulled off something pretty playable.

With default settings, the center of your screen is taken up by a large aiming
circle. You have auto-aim within this circle. So all you have to do is put
enemies in the ballpark of the center of the screen and pull the trigger. This
works effectively like extremely heavy aim assist, but without moving your
camera for you at all. As someone who gets motion sick in games easily and is
also bad at aiming with a controller, I greatly appreciated this. You can also
tweak the settings to make it a standard 3rd person shooter with no aim assist,
or crank the aim assist higher and force it to actually move your camera for
you. I found the default settings worked perfectly for me.

Auto-aim doesn't mean auto-hit, however. This is an RPG shooter, after all. Mass
Effect released a year later and did the same thing: your shots have random
spread that tightens as your stats or weapon-skills get higher. This makes the
gameplay not a pure 3rd person shooter, as your character's build and
progression can have as much if not more of an impact on your accuracy than your
skill with a controller. Again, for someone like me, that works out pretty well.
In fact, I'd say that the combat in this game plays out at a _similar_ (not
exact) quality as the original Mass Effect. Some people claim this game is
unplayable trash that barely functions, while Mass Effect is legendary. I think
one is given significantly more grace for unfair reasons.

You also have a mana bar with which you can cast spells through your equipped
materia. I liked this system. The materia acted essentially like under-barrel
attachments for your guns. A fire spell acts like a grenade launcher, for
example, while thunder acts like a shotgun blast. However, equipping materia to
a weapon makes it slower, so I didn't want to have a materia equipped at all
times. Instead, I put materia on my dedicated heavy weapon setups, and switched
to them as needed in combat.

That brings me to playstyle customization, which I was fairly satisfied with.
You mostly customize your guns---upgrading parts, swapping parts out, and so on.
You can even customize mid-mission. For example, if you left an area full of
tight corners and entered a wide open area with long angles, you might want to
stop for a brief moment to put a sniper scope on your rifle. This rapid
customization made Vincent feel like a badass firearm expert, adapting on the
fly as the situation demands.

You can hold up to three customized weapons at a time, and switch between them
in combat at the press of a button. This allowed for decent gameplay variety and
furthered Vincent's adaptability even more. I was slightly disappointed to find
that you can't switch out empty weapons. You have to reload first. It would have
been nice to switch on empty to keep firing faster than a reload would allow.
But it's a minor thing overall.

To encourage you to use those three weapons, there are three types of
ammunition. Running low on one? Switch to another! In fact, resource management
is strict enough that often you'll want to switch to one of your guns if its
ammo type is getting too full, to avoid being unable to pick up free ammo drops.
Unfortunately, this is a bit weirdly balanced, since you get similar amounts of
ammo for fast firing and slow firing weapons. This strongly weights the balance
towards slower, heavier weapons. Customization amplifies this. Sure I could
attach a short barrel to my pistol to move faster in combat and fire faster, but
doing so would reduce my ammo economy, forcing me to spend more money on ammo
which I could have spent on persistent upgrades. I disliked this, because it
felt like the obviously best way to play, but I vastly preferred the feeling of
playing the game with lighter weapons.

This resource pressure hurt the gameplay experience in other ways, too. My main
issue with this is that they turned Limit Break into a consumable item.
Vincent's Limit Break is the ability to temporarily transform into a monster. He
enters a super-powered state, taking very little damage and dealing lots of it,
especially in melee. This only lasts for about 30 seconds or so, but it's
extremely satisfying and fun to use. Unfortunately, you get very few of this
item throughout the entire game, and only by exploring levels off the beaten
path. I saved all of mine for boss fights. Lame.

I think the resource system was designed to encourage players to explore the
levels. Several other systems are like this. For example, there are multiple
kinds of collectibles that unlock "extras" in the main menu. The problem is,
this story never feels like a _stop and smell the roses_ experience. There's
always some deadly threat with lives on the line and huge responsibility on your
shoulders. Stopping to hunt for loot and collectibles runs directly counter to
the tone of the story. I think they only did this because other games do it,
without considering why those other games did it. This game clearly took a lot
of inspiration from Resident Evil, especially 4, but this is not a horror game.
Leaning into the action by ignoring resource constraints would have made the
game significantly more fun. It's not like Vincent tracked ammo in the original
game.

That isn't the only way the gameplay feels held back. During many cutscenes,
Vincent is shown leaping hundreds of feet and performing acts of superhuman
speed and strength. He can perform such over the top acrobatics that he may as
well have the ability to fly. In some scenes he even has a hover board for some
reason. Then the gameplay loads and you slowly walk around on the ground without
so much as a sprint button. I see what they were going for, making a shooter
game about the character with the most guns, but this feels like a genre in
search of a character instead of the other way around. Like, they wanted to make
a shooter game, and stapled FFVII to it to get it greenlit.

Boss fights ranged from absolutely terrible to pretty decent, depending on how
enjoyable the gimmick was. In a particularly hilarious example, a boss could not
be damaged in any way other than explosive barrels---which conveniently covered
the entire boss arena to a trivializing degree despite not having been present
in the preceding cutscene. But not having a gimmick at all was even worse. Some
bosses were just health bars with legs that run at you until you strafe them
long enough to kill them. Honestly, I had fun as long as I found the challenge
satisfying. One of the final fights of the game was fun for me because it pushed
me down to the wire so thoroughly. I had to sell every single piece of equipment
I was not actively using to afford all the resources I dumped into that fight. I
beat it with only 3 bullets left, no remaining consumables of any kind, and no
money. I was so satisfied when I pulled it off.

The game ends with a classic that I love every time: a permanent super-powered
form that you play as for the final few levels and final boss, with rock music
blasting in the background and some of the craziest spectacle in the game
surrounding you. Is the writing cheesy as all hell? Yes. Is every moment of it
entirely narratively unearned? Also yes. Did it still work on me? Absolutely. Do
I give the game points for this? A tiny bit.

I think the game makes the strongest impression at the start. I had a great time
with the first few levels and the first few bosses. The enemies weren't super
tanky yet. The production quality was high. The level design was fun and
interactive. For example, I clearly remember picking up a sniper scope for the
first time, and just a short distance away was a group of civilians I could only
save by sniping nearby enemies with that scope. The game drew no attention to
that, and left it entirely up to me to notice and save them. Really cool. There
were also lots of optional objectives early on, which were introduced in natural
and interesting ways.

Unfortunately, later on the levels got longer and more repetitive, the enemies
got tankier, the side objectives started to feel like secret main objectives,
and the bosses got worse and worse. The worst level design sins were the card
key gates. These tiny laser gates were _everywhere_ and frustrated me every
time. The keys were surprisingly easy to lose track of, and many were designed
to force you to backtrack over the same part of the level repeatedly. It didn't
help that the game was often unclear about where you are supposed to go and what
you're supposed to do.

One particular level baffled me. It was a stealth mission in which you play as
Cait Sith, but you're somehow pathetically weak and can't damage the basic
Shinra guards in any way, despite having canonically beaten the toughest threats
in the world prior to this. I assume this was done to force stealth. However,
there were no punishments for failing stealth other than taking some damage. So
I just ran through the whole thing and beat it in about 2 and a half minutes. At
least it wasn't long.

However, there were some great levels in there. My favorite was Shinra Manor. It
was cool to explore that place in 3D. Every time I turned a corner and
recognized a room, I was delighted. I got the most pleasant form of déjà vu
throughout that place, and it felt rewarding to already know my way around.
There was also a level near the end of the game || Deepground|| that was full
of very interesting spectacle and mystery.

Overall, this gameplay was fine. I don't know why it has such a bad reputation.
There were some frustrating parts, but there were also good parts. Mostly, it
was just barely above acceptable for long stretches. But there's apparently
nothing more terrible to online gamers than a 7/10.

## Music

This is my favorite part of the game. I've added a significant part of this
game's soundtrack to my playlists of Final Fantasy music. Masashi Hamauzu is one
of my favorite composers. I somehow had no idea he did music for this game,
which made it such a pleasant surprise when I recognized his style _immediately._

Instead of going into detail on why I love Hamauzu's music so much (I've already
lost count of the number of times I've done that), [I've compiled a list of my
favorite tracks.](https://music.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLfhCH6qzn4nQ)

There is another artist on this soundtrack, though. Gackt is an extremely
popular rock and pop icon in Japan. He wrote the song for the final level,
[LONGING](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=wis0CaoJ4lE), and the game's main
theme, [REDEMPTION](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=q9zpMg0txIQ). He also
provided a voice and his likeness for a character in this game. More on that
later in the story section, but honestly not much more.

Though I'm keeping this music section short, I want to emphasize that I enjoyed
this music so much that it saved this game from receiving a much worse score
from me. It's that good, no notes.

## Story

Unfortunately, this is where my enjoyment of this game drops off a cliff.

Due to this taking place after the original game, any spoilers in this section
are also spoilers for the original and the entire Remake series.

I was excited to play this for the story content. Vincent did not get a ton of
detail in the original game, despite fitting into a very important place in the
world. || Lucrecia and Hojo are the parents of Sephiroth, and Vincent's
backstory is about that whole situation.|| There are plenty of interesting
possibilities to work with there. They're all squandered.

This game spends most of its time with Vincent's backstory just repeating the
parts we already know. It repeats the _same things_ almost every time. I lost
count of how many times Lucrecia said "I'm so sorry" without adding anything
new. It technically adds some new stuff, like where Vincent's final Limit Break
came from and what his dad looks like---but all of that stuff is bad. None of it
adds anything interesting to the original story. No new parallels are drawn. No
interesting themes are expanded upon. It's just a lore dump of random stuff that
only interacts with itself and adds nothing thematically interesting. We did get
some cool scenes of body horror out of it at least. So it has that going for it.

This story has three other major elements: || Omega/Chaos, Deepground, and
Shelke/Shalua.|| All of them suck. If you need to skip for spoiler reasons, you
are not missing anything of value.

|| Deepground is the main antagonist force in this story. They are a group of
Shinra super soldiers made after the SOLDIER project failed. Rather than being
implanted with Jenova cells and then infused with Mako energy, they are infused
with significantly higher quantities of pure light Mako energy. As such, they
are a sort of mirror of SOLDIER. I like that quality about them, at least.
There's interesting potential there. Unfortunately they are mirrors of SOLDIER
in every other way, too. These characters suck and their character designs suck.
They are walking tropes with ugly designs that look pulled from a completely
different sci-fi universe. Their motivations also suck, which brings me to
Omega.||

|| Omega is a Weapon of the Planet. It's purpose is to gather up the Planet's
Lifestream and move on to another planet if the Planet is ever threatened with
destruction. It detects this by a sudden and massive influx of Lifestream,
representing apocalyptic loss of life. This is Deepground's aim, which doesn't
make a lot of sense for them. It's later explained that Hojo's digital
consciousness was manipulating them to create a perfect vessel for him, with
which he intends to claim the power of Omega for himself. Also, only "untainted"
Lifestream can trigger this, so Deepground only need those who never had
Geostigma. Yet, somehow darkness counts as tainted Lifestream, and Nero gained
his darkness abilities from the pure Lifestream? Nothing is consistent.||

|| There are two terrible retcons here. First, with Sephiroth's plan to cause an
apocalypse to force the planet to gather its Lifestream in a single place, where
he would absorb it all, how did this never come up? With the wisdom of the
Ancients and Shinra's own apparent knowledge about these topics, nobody ever
thought of this? It doesn't make sense. Further, it directly contradicts the
themes of the original game if the Planet would always survive and just move on
to another world. This would make the Planet act like Jenova! The other retcon
is that Aerith gave the world a cure for the Geostigma in Advent Children, and
that was very importantly a permanent thing. If everyone who ever had it is
permanently tainted, all hope is lost. Sephiroth will inevitably win, if this is
how things work. There is no other outcome possible. These elements _only undo_
the positive elements of the other stories so far! ||

|| How Vincent fits into this is that his final Limit Break, Chaos, is the
reaper meant to cause all this death to bring about Omega. There is a single
materia, the Protomateria, which allows control over them both, but only one at
a time. This was _somehow_ found by Lucrecia (unexplained) and embedded in
Vincent's body during all that experimentation, in order to prevent Chaos (which
she used to save his life) from controlling him. I don't think Chaos needed an
explanation in the first place. None of his other Limit Breaks have any
explanation either. Once again, all of this adds nothing to Vincent's character.
It's just something that interacts with this story alone, takes care of its own
problem, and is just an excuse for Vincent to be involved.||

|| The final element is the relationship between Shelke and Shalua. Shalua was
technically a character from an earlier entry in the Compilation. She appeared
for one whole scene in Before Crisis! Wow! This was planned in advance, because
Shalua has a character portrait in that game unlike most NPCs, an honor reserved
for characters important to the world and overarching story. It was weird that
she had this distinction, despite being such a tiny background character in that
game, but now it makes sense: she was being introduced for this game. Anyways,
she is back as Punished Venom Shalua. Offscreen, she's nearly died so many times
that she's almost a cyborg with how many artificial body parts she has. The
whole time, she was looking for her sister, Shelke, who turns out to be a member
of Deepground. Somehow Shelke has the body of a child but her mind continued to
age, despite this not happening to any of the other characters. Fucking hate
that! The game tries _really hard_ to make these two characters the emotional
core of the game. It does not work. They just dump their whole life stories on
you during your first interactions with them, then bad stuff happens to them
five minutes later as if you'd automatically be emotionally attached to them. ||

I feel like it's one of the great sins of sequels to focus mostly on new
characters that don't interact at all with the original plot. At that point, why
even make this tied to the original work? This game does that constantly, and
really feels like it shouldn't have been a Final Fantasy VII game. The only
thing making it a Final Fantasy VII game is that some of the characters have the
same names and character models. They don't even act like their original
versions or progress their original stories in any way. It's a Final Fantasy VII
game only because the creators wanted it to sell better. It leeches off the
original and actively harms its world.

There's a secret ending to this game, unlocked if you find all of the "G Report"
collectibles scattered throughout. While talking with NPCs, you'll hear many
times about a "mysterious SOLDIER named G," but there are never any details.
Even in this secret ending, there are no details. This SOLDIER, G, shows up and
says a couple lines, clearly voiced by and bearing the likeness of Gackt. He
saves one of the main villains of the game, ||summons a single black wing
exactly like Sephiroth's||, refuses to elaborate, and leaves. _Find out more in
the next game!_ There was never a next game. At least, never a game that showed
where he went after this. He was clearly being set up to be the major threat of
another story taking place after this, but that never happened. This is a post
credits scene teasing a project that didn't yet exist. In fact, it feels like
they didn't even have an idea of what that project _would be_ if it was
greenlit. They just added a bunch of loose ends that could potentially be picked
up later, generated from a bunch of random nonsense.

I do not like this writing. At the start of this review, I was leaning toward a
7/10. The more I thought about the story, the more it dropped.

## In the Compilation

What did this take from, and bring to, the Compilation? As a reminder, here's
the list of major Compilation common elements so far:

- The art style has shifted to a more realistic, but stylish aesthetic.
- There’s a much greater tendency to leave things unexplained and drop vague
  hints at things, to tease and connect to other Compilation works.
- The Lifestream has light and dark parts. In general, light and darkness are
  now more of a focus than mankind’s hubris against nature.
- There is a much stronger focus on action, especially over-the-top, superhuman,
  acrobatic combat and slicing through buildings.
- There is a stronger focus on Zack, AVALANCHE, the Turks, and party members
  that didn't get as much love in the original.
- The Lifestream can grant premonitions now.
- The technology level of the world is significantly modernized.
- There are super soldier programs other than SOLDIER. Some of these are more
  successful than others. All are traumatizing.
- There are more “special” materia out there than just Meteor and Holy.
- Sephiroth has that iconic new one-winged-angel look.
- Cloud is slightly more of a brooding loner.
- Characters who died are brought back through retcons, gotchas, and all new
  lore invented for the sole purpose of bringing them back.

The new art style and action style continue, including a modernization of the
technology level of the world. This game actually takes the technology much
further into cyberpunk, and the art style is darker and grittier than ever.
There are super soldier programs other than SOLDIER, and this game introduces
one of its own. The Lifestream has light and dark parts, though this one made
that way more vague and confusing, contradicting itself. There are even _more_
special materia out there, as this game once again introduces one of its own.
Sephiroth doesn't really appear in this one, but || G has that wing||. Cloud
barely appears, but does seem quiet as expected.

There's a stronger focus on the characters that didn't get much in the
original---this time Vincent, Yuffie, and Cait Sith. Some of those got better
treatment than others. Vincent was unfortunately a mixed bag. I enjoy his new
character design, but the Chaos stuff is pretty bad and his personality is
mostly flat since he spends most of his time brooding. They somehow managed to
get me to hate Cait Sith even more. There's a really dumb scene in which Reeve
gets shot to death, only for Cait Sith to reveal that Reeve was a robot that
Cait Sith was controlling. They did it. They made Cait Sith even more confusing
and removed even the last, tiniest scraps of relatability the character had.
Fortunately, Yuffie was handled well. She's a lovable little goofball in this
game and I love her for that. Though I wish they kept her original character
design.

I was consistently annoyed with how frequently this game trivialized death.
There's this whole deal with digital consciousnesses, allowing theoretically
anyone to come back as long as it can be retconned that they had their brain
scanned in the past. || This happens to both Hojo and Lucrecia, both of the
important characters from Vincent's past. While I like the symmetry of that, I
think this story would have been more impactful if they stayed dead. There could
be an interesting element if Vincent, as the reaper Chaos, had to kill both his
personal villain and lost love in order to save the world, but Lucrecia survives
anyways!|| They even tease that ||Shalua|| could return in the future, negating
her sacrifice. Really, I should have seen it coming when that Reeve-getting-shot
scene happened at the start of the game. It really set the tone for how this
story would unfold: constant walk-backs and zero consequences.

The _biggest_ Compilation-ism of this game is its obsession with creating
connections to other Compilation entries so that you have to play them all to
get the full experience. I already mentioned how Shalua was technically a minor
character in another game that they tried _very_ hard to make players care about
in this one. It also brings up Geostigma from Advent Children for seemingly no
reason other than to tie to it, and all characters use their costumes from that
movie. You also use the airship from that movie, the Shera. However, Dirge of
Cerberus takes all of this further by attempting to create as many tie points as
it can for future entries. That airship is apparently ancient technology from
some advanced civilization we've somehow never heard of before, despite having
visited the most important ancient sites on the Planet. There are a bunch of
characters introduced to the world that have earth-shattering lore implications
but seem to be invented on the back of a napkin with the expectation that a
future entry would fill in the gaps.

# Final Thoughts

**Rating: 5/10**  
_Playtime: 10 hours_

Overall, this is not a bad game. It's fine. I would not call it the worst Final
Fantasy. I wouldn't even put it in the bottom five, and all the worse games are
mainline entries! However, I don't blame anyone for disliking this game. If
you're mainly here for story, the story sucks. If the gameplay didn't happen to
work for someone, there's not much left to enjoy. I just happen to be the
particular kind of sicko to enjoy this combat somewhat, and the music carried it
the rest of the way to an enjoyable experience. However, I still found the story
so offensively bad that I can't give the game any higher rating than this.

Unfortunately, the Compilation is not off to a very strong start.
