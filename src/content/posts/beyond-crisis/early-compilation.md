With this post I am kicking off a retrospective on the Compilation of FFVII, the
largest expanded universe in the Final Fantasy Franchise, starting with the
earliest miscellaneous multimedia releases.

The Compilation of FFVII began with the announcement of Advent Children, a movie
serving as a sequel to FFVII, in 2003. It is no coincidence that this was the
same year Square merged with Enix, FFX-2 (the first sequel in the series)
released, and Hironobu Sakaguchi left to form his own studio. The company was
headed in a new direction.

Sakaguchi had a core philosophy for making games that was part of every prior
Final Fantasy: nothing should be saved for sequels. He thought that no idea
should be left out of a game. Every game should be a complete picture relying on
nothing that came before or after it. He has gone on to say in interviews that
he outright hates sequels of any kind.

The year 2003 therefore marked a massive turning point for Final Fantasy. The
projects immediately following were _full_ of sequels, prequels, and all the
other -quels. FFXI would be an MMO with expansion sequels. FFXII is a prequel to
FF Tactics (sorta). FFXIII and FFXV were originally intended to be part of a
trilogy of games with a shared mythology, and FFXIII ended up spawning its own
sequel trilogy. Before all of that, the largest and longest running set of
-quells began: The Compilation of FFVII. It is a collection of games, movies,
books, anime, and short stories that expand and explore the universe of Final
Fantasy VII. It is still ongoing today, as the FFVII Remake series is included.

The Compilation is, at least how I read it, Yoshinori Kitase, Tetsuya Nomura,
and Kazushige Nojima, unleashed from the constraints Hironobu Sakaguchi had
previously imposed upon them, for better or worse. Though Sakaguchi was heavily
involved in the original FFVII, he stepped away for FFVIII and let those three
take the lead. It seems he did not like the result, and made FFIX as a grand
overcorrection back to the roots of the series, rejecting as much of the new
style of Final Fantasy as he could. Kitase, Nomura, and Nojima designed much
more "wild" games than Sakaguchi. Their games are full of crazy twists and
convoluted plots often involving time travel, parallel worlds, and such. They
also have a particular aesthetic and tone to them that differs greatly from
Sakaguchi's---they are more realistic in appearance yet larger than life in
every possible way, as opposed to Sakaguchi's preference to make everything look
like dolls on dioramas. The Compilation expands on the world of FFVII, and paves
over Sakaguchi's influence on it.

For some fans, that was a terrible betrayal, as Sakaguchi **is** Final Fantasy
to them. Many fans simply do not like any Final Fantasy that released after
Sakaguchi stopped working on them. Not out of spite, but because the thing they
loved most about the games was the particular flavor he brought to them. For
those fans, seeing a world they loved stripped of the core piece of its identity
they loved most made them extremely upset. Especially when it seemed like Square
Enix was suddenly making moves driven far more by money than ever before. I
think that's valid.

On the other hand, there are some fans of the series that _enjoy_ the new
direction Final Fantasy took after the merger in 2003. Even earlier, there were
fans that enjoyed Final Fantasy VIII, and wanted more from that set of
developers. I fall into this camp. I've even said previously that I wanted to
see more work from Kitase, Nomura, and Nojima, specifically. It turns out it was
here in the Compilation all along. With the way my Final Fantasy ranking is
going, I'm starting to think I liked some of the early entires in the series
somewhat _in spite of_ Sakaguchi, not because of him.

I think that will be the major deciding factor in whether or not someone enjoys
the Compilation content: Are you a Sakaguchi fan or a Nomura fan? The answer can
be both, of course, in which case the Compilation is likely to be much more of a
mixed bag. Well, it's probably always going to be mixed. From what I've heard
(and the small bit I've already experienced), the quality of the Compilation
content varies quite a lot.

There were, of course, many more names involved than just Nomura, Nojima, and
Kitase. These three just seem to be the most influential in this project and the
driving force behind it. So I've used them as a convenient bit of shorthand for
this "new" set of Final Fantasy creative leads. To be fair, there are also
plenty more names behind Sakaguchi, but he too is constantly used as shorthand
for a certain form of Final Fantasy.

In the spirit of the Compilation, I've decided to spin off a review series from
my main Final Fantasy Retrospective. I've named it Beyond Crisis, after the
Compilation's tendency to use the word "Crisis" in its titles to refer to the
events of the original FFVII. Since this is an exploration beyond FFVII, it felt
fitting. Though these entries will not be part of the Final Fantasy
Retrospective, I'll still include them in my series ranking.

This first post will cover several early entries in the Compilation that are too
small to have individual posts. Since they also released in the early days of
the Compilation, it seemed easy enough to line them all up and knock them out
together. It felt to me like these early entries were works of exploration into
what the Compilation would eventually become. There are some mistakes that got
walked back, several directions that were abandoned, and so on. By analyzing
them all together, we can better predict the trajectory of this overarching
metaseries.

These entries are listed in release order, and were only a few years apart.

# Kingdom Hearts

This technically isn't part of the Compilation of Final Fantasy VII, but I still
think it's worth mentioning here. Kingdom Hearts released a few years before the
Compilation started, and it is very much its own series. However, the
Compilation creators were all in leadership positions for this game, and it
featured quite a few characters from FFVII. This was the first time any Final
Fantasy characters had shown up in another game, and it had a huge impact on how
those characters were handled in the future.

The biggest and most obvious influence that Kingdom Hearts had on the
Compilation was Sephiroth's character design. Specifically, the design with one
black angel wing on his right side. This has now become his most iconic design,
but it never actually showed up in the original game. It was invented here. More
generally, angel wings and scattered feathers are frequent motifs in the
compilation, but started here.

This is a bit more of a stretch, because Square Enix was moving in this
direction in general, but I also see this as a point of transition for how these
characters are visualized when it comes to action. Not only was this one of the
earliest and most impactful action RPGs released by Square Enix, the characters
were shown performing incredible, superhuman feats of acrobatics and speed
during both gameplay and cutscenes. Characters could leap high into the air and
defy physics as they blocked or attacked while seeming to float in place, or
even exert effort to magically spin in mid-air to swing a weapon. I say that not
to disparage this style---in fact, I love it---but because it is a marked change
from the original.

The original game was made of abstraction on top of abstraction, so it's hard to
know exactly what the designers were imagining during its creation. Turn based
combat where both sides stood in a line and patiently waited for each other's
animations to play out was not a diegetic element. It should be assumed that
battles actually look like something else. The traversal of the world map and
several other things are similar abstractions. However, I think it was unlikely
that this superhuman level of acrobatics was the original intention. Why climb a
mountain if you can leap over it? Why bother with parachutes if you can safely
fall hundreds of feet? This over-the-top, aerial action is one of the many
stylistic changes of the Compilation, and I think there's an argument it started
here.

There are a few more (minor) things: This was the first title to correct Aeris
to Aerith, which has been kept consistent ever since. This game cemented
One-Winged Angel as Sephiroth's theme, when it was originally only his final
boss theme and he had a different motif throughout the rest of the game.
Finally, the characterization of Cloud was changed to slightly more of a
conflicted, brooding, loner type. It even drew parallels between Cloud and
Vincent in order to accomplish this, giving Cloud a cape like Vincent's and a
wing reminiscent of Vincent's final Limit Break.

# Before Crisis

While technically Advent Children was announced first, Before Crisis was the
first entry in the Compilation to release. It's actually important that it did,
because characters and concepts from this entry are referenced frequently by the
other early parts of the Compilation.

Unfortunately, this game is dead. One of the deadest Final Fantasy games ever.
It was exclusive to cell phones, and never made its way out of Japan. It also
made use of common cell phone features, such as the on-device camera and cell
service. These features would be hard to fake. Not only is it built for phones
nobody uses anymore and there are no emulators for, the game released in a
rather odd format. Individual chapters had to be downloaded separately. So even
with a working game installation on a working device, the chapters could no
longer be downloaded after the servers shut down.

There is a fan-made RPG Maker port of the game that released a handful of years
ago, but as far I know it is an experience full of compromises. The gameplay is
basic to the point of being essentially a visual novel interrupted by bare
bones, extremely slow combat. Some who have played it said they would have
preferred the gameplay removed in favor of a real visual novel for a more
streamlined experience. Additionally, the the original game had ten playable
characters, each with slightly different stories, and this remake chose one of
those. The remake was also based on other fan translations, pieced together from
bits and pieces of recordings of the original game. I have huge respect for the
creator of this remake, and all the effort that went into it. It's a great work
of restoration, but since the story is the only thing I care about, YouTube
videos with fan translations are good enough for me.

The game is about to die again! Ever Crisis is a gacha game that is shutting
down this year. It retells the other stories of the Compilation. The last story
to be brought into it, literally a single month before it shuts down forever, is
Before Crisis. I'm sure someone will better preserve the story this time around,
and we'll finally get a more accurate read on which parts of this story Square
Enix consider canon. It is, however, still hilarious to me that Square Enix is
killing this game _twice_, through mobile game releases both times.

Technically, that's right around the corner, and I could wait and play that
version. However, grinding through an entire gacha game just to play this story
sounds like a lot of work. Maybe when it's out, someone will make a comparison
of what changed (from the actual original, or even just the community's
collective understanding of it) if anything, and I'll catch up on that. For now,
I'm going to write about this not as a review, but as a summary of what it does
for the Compilation.

This game takes place before the events of FFVII, and partially during it.
Technically, some of the game takes place during and after the || Nibelheim
incident || of FFVII. It dives deep into the history of the Turks and AVALANCHE,
setting them up for the start of FFVII. Some of this stuff was hinted at in the
original material, but most of it was just left open as "there's more history
here, but no time to explain." Out of everything in the original, those factions
were what I thought could have used the most additional fleshing out, so I'm
glad they started here.

I'm going to give a **very** bare bones plot recap here, and then dig into what
elements were brought forward into the Compilation. I'm mostly skipping over
cameos of the original cast and references to the events of the original game.
There are no spoilers for the original game or the Remake series here.

|| Turns out, there used to be _way_ more Turks. They mostly all die during the
events of this game. You play as the Turks in this game, and AVALANCHE are your
enemies. This is an interesting inversion of the original game, but it turns out
that AVALANCHE were pretty fucked up back then! The "Old Guard" members want to
wipe humans out for the sake of the Planet. To do so, they attempt to summon
Zirconiade Weapon, which necessitated embedding materia into their troops and
draining their life force without their knowledge. The Weapon is summoned, and
most of the Turks die in this battle, but your chosen protagonist unlocks a
power-of-friendship limit break to defeat it. ||

|| Along the way, a few things are retconned to get this story linked up with
the first. This is done delicately, at least, always leaving these characters
out of sight from where they could have been seen in the original. The Turks are
stealthy assassins and secret agent types, after all. Basically any time Shinra
vaguely does something in the background with a fade to black, these guys were
involved somewhere. I appreciate how that was done. It doesn't mess with the
original too much, and allows this game to revisit some of the best scenes in
the original in a much more natural and interesting way. The biggest twist is
something that was vaguely hinted at in the original game but never stated:
Rufus Shinra was an influential member of early AVALANCHE, possibly even their
founder. He fed them information and technology for the cause. The dark result
of this was President Shinra ordering most Turks killed to cover this up for
Rufus. This changes how I see their dynamic. ||

So, what does this do for the Compilation, and how does it expand the world of
the original game? Primarily it expands on those two factions, which I think is
the best improvement to my enjoyment of the original world. When I play the
original and the Remake series, I'll think of the Turks and AVALANCHE
differently, in an interesting way that adds texture to the world. Though they
are not a part of the original directly, this game also gave us a bunch of extra
Turk and AVALANCHE characters that can be utilized by the future Compilation
entries. Finally, this introduces the idea of || the existence of super soldier
programs other than SOLDIER. After all, there are many other kinds of magic and
fantasy in this world, all of which would probably be explored by Shinra's
fucked up scientists. || It also introduced the idea that there are more unique,
powerful materia out there than just Holy and Meteor. Finally, it significantly
modernized the technology level of the world, from a dieselpunk setting in which
the most modern things were chunky CRTs, to something closer to cyberpunk with a
world wide web and a cell phone in every pocket.

# Advent Children

This is the most important of all the entries here. This movie is what truly
kicked off the Compilation of FFVII and defined the tone of the series going
forward, though it was still influenced by what came before it.

Advent Children takes place after the events of the original game. As such, the
spoiler tags of this section may contain spoilers for the original game and the
entire Remake series.

> This Reunion is for you.

That is the opening line of the movie, addressed to "those who loved this world
and knew friendly company therein." And so the tone is set: This one's for the
fans. It's an excuse to get the characters back on screen, to see them in a way
they've never been seen before, and to pour as much fanservice as possible
directly into your eyes and ears.

That fanservice-oriented nature is very clear throughout the movie. The script
is not... particularly good. There are some lines that are still made fun of
over 20 years later, such as "I've thought of a wonderful present for you. Shall
I give you despair?" It's constant nonsense and the characters say things
seemingly just to fill space and _finally give these characters a voice._ Though
ironically many of these voices would be quickly replaced. That said, it didn't
really set out with any other goal than to give these characters an excuse to
fight together again, and in that sense it succeeded. This is a comfort-food
type movie.

Fortunately, it's actually pretty funny. Even on a rewatch, I've found myself
laughing along to the very well executed bits. It helps that these characters
have already been built up, and the original game had a good sense of humor to
begin with. The history behind these new interactions between these characters
lets them punch above their comedic weight.

Instead of analyzing this on its narrative merits, I'll focus on what it brought
to the Compilation and what influence it had on future interpretations of the
original game.

First and most obvious: The character designs have been reinterpreted and
evolved. The original game had a few different designs for each character, split
between combat, FMV cutscenes, and everything else. Though the former two were
more realistically proportioned and higher fidelity than the blocky, chibi
models the game is known for, they still kinda looked like toys. Advent Children
was aiming for a more realistic style, and alongside all the other aesthetic
changes, opted to redesign all the characters from the ground up based on their
original designs. These are the designs that came to be used for every
Compilation project that came after. Chances are that, unless you played the
original decades ago, when you think of these characters you'll be picturing
these designs or designs based off of them, not the original designs.

Not all of the character designs were awesome, however. I strongly dislike how
they handled Barret. Not only did they give him a mesh shirt and a prosthetic
hand to replace his gun, they made him drill for oil! The very thing he spent
his life rallying against was an analogy for oil---and now not only does oil
_also_ exist in this world, Barret is massively in favor of it? That kind of
characterization flub is unfortunately common in this movie. Thankfully, there
weren't many opportunities for these bad designs to get used before they were
quickly forgotten by future entries.

Alongside that aesthetic shift towards realism came a modernization of the world
of Final Fantasy VII. As mentioned above, this started with Before Crisis, but
Advent Children cemented that idea. Though ||humanity is still recovering||
after the events of the original game, everyone has a cell phone in their pocket
and perfect cell service everywhere. A lot of time is spent on how cool flip
phones are. The customized phones of several characters are shown, and there's
even a gag where a cell phone plays the FFVII victory theme as its ring tone
directly after a fight ends.

Interestingly, the more realistic, modern style is accompanied by _much_ less
realistic action. This story was always fantasy, but in this movie seemingly
everyone can leap hundreds of feet and tank the force of being hit by a bus
without issue. Everyone also seems to have like, a 3D rotation muscle they can
flex to spin themselves while falling through the air, with enough force to
swing weapons. Characters simultaneously have zero mass, zero air friction, and
strike with the force of ten gorillas.

Admittedly, this often works pretty well for me. I can just sit back and enjoy
the spectacle. It's not supposed to be serious, and Final Fantasy VII was never
really about hard magic or power-scaling or anything like that anyways. However,
sometimes it can go a bit too far. One of my favorite scenes in the movie
(because of how STUPID it is) involves each other party member jumping hundreds
of feet in the air, to meet Cloud there and launch him higher and faster like a
multi-stage friendship rocket. This fight doesn't even have any real plot
relevance (even by this movie's standard), it's just an excuse to lead up to
this moment. The rest of the party fades into the background for the rest of the
movie after this, taking up the role of Dragon Ball Z fight commentators on the
sidelines, explaining what's going on in Cloud's head so he doesn't have to say
it.

Another major goal of this movie was to set up the future Compilation entries
with new hooks and ideas by only briefly mentioning them here. This is that
"Nomura vs Sakaguchi" thing. For example, instead of using the iconic Highwind
airship from the game, Cid shows up in an all new airship he calls "The Shera."
It's clearly a _very_ different design than the Highwind, perhaps even alien,
but we only see it for a few seconds. Why introduce that at all, instead of just
using the old airship fans would obviously love to see on the big screen?
Because this new ship would become a plot point in future content. Similarly,
Vincent and Zack get a bunch of attention, because they are the protagonists of
a couple games that would release soon after this movie did.

Advent Children, as mentioned earlier, was influenced by the works that came
before it. It took a few existing elements and doubled down on them, making them
an even more solid part of the Compilation. The antagonists embed materia into
their bodies, much like Before Crisis. The Kingdom Hearts design for Sephiroth
was also used in this movie for a brief moment. Similarly returning from Kingdom
Hearts, Cloud is lonelier and more similar to Vincent, to the point that he is
symbolized multiple times as a literal lone wolf. And of course, we also get
more time with the fan-favorite Turks, though not as in-depth as Before Crisis.

It's a little hard to tell exactly how Advent Children fits into the canon of
the series or what it brings to the Compilation, since it never stops to explain
anything. We know that it is considered canon to the Remake series, thanks to
confirmation by its directors, but we still don't know how a lot of the elements
of this movie _actually work._ I'm hoping the Remake series will help explain it
before it concludes.

|| One thing that's certainly new and carries on into the future is the
existence of a dark Lifestream, or at least pockets of darkness within it. It is
not yet clear if this is Sephiroth and/or Jenova's corruption of the Lifestream,
the souls of tainted/evil individuals in the Lifestream, or maybe some
combination? The movie contradicts itself and the original game, or at least
seems to with how vague it is. ||

|| Multiple characters claim that the Geostigma disease is the result of someone
being infected with the "Sephiroth gene" through contact with the contaminated
Lifestream. However, this is _not_ a physical infection of Jenova cells, as the
titular Advent Children need Jenova cells to become whole, so they obviously
don't already have them. Yet, those infected with Geostigma become similar to
the failed Sephiroth clones of the original game, able to be overtaken by his
will and gaining supernatural speed and strength. It also appears that Geostigma
infection only happens to those who are in a state of despair, seeming to
actually symbolize that despair. Those who die with this
despair/gene/disease/whatever become part of the dark Lifestream, under
Sephiroth's control. ||

|| But if Sephiroth got his power from Jenova, how does all of this work
_explicitly and specifically without Jenova?_ He was not _actually_ of Ancient
blood. What power of his own does Sephiroth have? Is the implication that he
absorbed enough of the Lifestream at the end of the game to achieve a partial
godlike state that gave him control of some specific part of the Lifestream? Is
it that he made being an Ancient such a deep part of his identity that he
believed it hard enough to gain Ancient powers? This is never explained.
Sephiroth is just a new eldritch entity of his own now. I'm not sure how. But
clearly that idea is incredibly important to the Compilation going forward. ||

|| Cloud sees several visions through the Lifestream during this movie. In the
original game, he had episodes where it looked like he was going through mental
anguish and his grip on reality was slipping, but visions weren't part of it. He
goes through similar episodes here, but his physical reaction is toned down to
one of pain gripping his head and arm, and he much more clearly sees visions.
Also Aerith talks to him through the Lifestream, though these visions are
pleasant and comforting rather than painful like his other episodes. ||

|| The other thing this movie adds to the Compilation is the idea of Aerith as
an opposing Lifestream entity to Sephiroth. She is positioned as an entity of
purity and grace, welcoming and comforting souls while unburdening them of their
regrets. She is the embodiment of hope and forgiveness, while Sephiroth is the
embodiment of fear and spite. She even forgives the Advent Children when they
die, allowing them to merge into the Lifestream, and grants humanity a pool of
healing water that cures them of their Geostigma and therefore symbolically of
despair. ||

I don't think this is a particularly _good_ movie, but if you enjoy these
characters then it's worth watching at least once. Though you should watch the
Complete edition, which came out a few years later. I will cover that edition
and its changes in the future. While I don't think this enhances my enjoyment of
the original game significantly, it's clearly incredibly important and
influential for the Remake series. Keep your expectations low, throw it on when
you've got some space and want a chill, unserious experience, and enjoy the
silly ride.

# Last Order

This is an OVA that was initially only released in Japan, but eventually
released in other regions as a bonus in the collector's edition of Advent
Children. This single episode covers several in-game events as a series of
flashbacks. As such, it contains heavy spoilers for both the original game and
the entire Remake series.

I can talk a bit without getting into spoilers though. The main attraction of
this anime is the ability to see several iconic moments animated in this
beautiful style, complete with voice acting. These scenes were originally
presented with the chibi, blocky models, not even the nice FMV ones. So this is
a massive upgrade. Since Advent Children focused on the events after the
original game, it was pretty light on this kind of content. The other thing this
does is begin to connect the Compilation entries together. Some of the combat in
here uses the superhuman, incredibly acrobatic fight choreography that Advent
Children introduced. It also features the characters from Before Crisis,
tastefully and subtly inserted into the background of scenes they were retconned
into by that game. Unfortunately, there were other retcons that were _quite bad_
and had to be walked back in future Compilation entries. To get into that I have
to bring up spoilers for the entire Remake series and original game.

|| My main issues involve changes to the confrontation between Cloud and
Sephiroth. Cloud stabs Sephiroth in the front instead of the back, which both
makes less sense due to Sephiroth not being fast enough to block it, and also
makes Sephiroth's description of Cloud as a back stabber less literal, when that
literalness was clearly originally intentional and is even referenced by
Sephiroth stabbing Aerith in the back. The biggest issue is that instead of
Cloud managing to kill Sephiroth, he just wounds him and then Sephiroth decides
to kill himself for no reason. He just turns and jumps off the edge. I'm baffled
by how bad that change is. There are a few other minor changes like Tifa finding
her father _outside_ the reactor instead of inside, but those are minor in
comparison. The very final moment is a retcon of how Zack dies, which is also
puzzling and unnecessary, especially since it breaks continuity with Advent
Children. ||

Overall I think this is the least worth experiencing so far. It doesn't really
add anything to the Compilation at this point. Crisis Core covers this content
better and more accurately, so there is no reason to go back for this now that
we have that. It was kinda nice to see the Compilation entries starting to come
together like that, but that's heavily outweighed by the terrible retcons. In
fact, some official sources have even struck it from the record of the
Compilation of Final Fantasy VII. Regardless, for the sake of completeness, I've
covered it.

# The Maiden Who Travels the Planet

This is a novella covering the the perspective of Aerith during moments of the
original game that we were not able to see. It was originally published as part
of the Final Fantasy VII Ultimania Omega, a guide book containing information on
the world and development history of Final Fantasy VII, as well as some
additional bonus content such as this.

Spoilers for the original game and the entire Remake series:

|| This is the story of Aerith's perspective of the game's main events from her
position in the Lifestream. She travels the world, helping souls that still have
regrets find peace so they can fully dissolve. The story focuses on the souls we
already knew, characters who died in the original game. For example, Biggs,
Wedge, and Jessie reveal that they took their bomb plans from the Old Guard
AVALANCHE, and regret the deaths they caused. Some are able to find redemption,
such as those three and Dyne, but some are shown to be unrepentant and evil to
their core like President Shinra and Hojo (who actually appears as his
Jenova-altered form, suggesting that the soul follows the body's shape). The
latter category of characters are dark clouds in the Lifestream, and get
absorbed by Sephiroth as he draws in power. ||

|| She is _extremely_ obsessed with Cloud in this story, to the point that it
feels like a fanfiction ship. Almost all of her thoughts are of Cloud. She says
she wanted to save the Planet for Cloud's sake. She never missed a moment to
think about how she's so, so, so, **so** jealous of Tifa. She did guide Tifa
through the Lifestream to Cloud, because her darling Cloud could only be healed
that way, of course. Though she was, again, so very jealous. She's oddly cold to
Zack, as if he was never even her _friend_, to say nothing of first love. She
didn't even know he was dead, despite him dying so close to Midgar. She was able
to detect the death of Elmyra's husband across the entire world, but nothing for
Zack? Feels like an odd retcon. This was clearly written with a hardcore
shipper's agenda. ||

I think the main thing this adds over the original that would become a part of
the future Compilation is that (spoilers up through Rebirth in the Remake
series) || she received some premonitions from the lifestream, including her own
death, rather than simply knowledge of the past and present. This is a bit of a
retcon of statements in the original game that state Aerith never intended to
die. ||

I think this is another one that can be safely skipped. For a bit more closure
on certain characters, it's not bad. But it doesn't add much to the Compilation
that sticks around. Most of it recaps bits that were already covered by other
Compilation entries. It feels like the focus of this story was to prop up Aerith
as the author's chosen waifu, more than anything else. Future official lists of
Compilation content no longer include it.

# Final Thoughts

While I don't consider any of these entries so far to be super high quality
content enjoyable purely on their own merits, the process of studying them
together and understanding the history of the development of the Compilation of
Final Fantasy VII has been an enjoyable experience for me. It's an interesting
historical exercise, digging into what was going on within Square Enix during
these years after the merger when Final Fantasy was entering a whole new era,
for better or worse.

So, what was the trajectory of the Compilation of Final Fantasy VII, before a
single game came out? Here are the elements I find significant:

- The art style has shifted to a more realistic, but stylish aesthetic.
- There's a much greater tendency to leave things unexplained and drop vague
  hints at things, to tease and connect to other Compilation works.
- The Lifestream has light and dark parts. In general, light and darkness are
  now more of a focus than mankind's hubris against nature.
- There is a much stronger focus on action, especially over-the-top, superhuman,
  acrobatic combat and slicing through buildings.
- There is a stronger focus on Vincent, Zack, AVALANCHE, and the Turks.
- The Lifestream can grant premonitions now.
