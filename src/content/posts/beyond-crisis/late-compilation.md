With games out of the way, the rest of the compilation expands on the world of
Final Fantasy VII by... doubling down on Advent Children?? The compilation was a
single movie followed by various forms of apology for it. Plus a few bad games,
as a treat.

This post covers _On the Way to a Smile_, _Advent Children Complete_, _The Kids
Are Alright: A Turks Side Story_, and the storyline of _The First Soldier_ that
was told within _Ever Crisis_. Plus a few small, related things.

# On the Way to a Smile

This book contains a set of short story prequels to _Advent Children_. It released
_effectively_ alongside the Complete edition of the movie. Both work together to
expand the original.

## Episode: Denzel

_Advent Children_ introduced a new character, Denzel, who featured prominently
in the movie. His presence or backstory were never really explained. He was just
there, and more important than most of the original cast for some reason. This
short story fills in those gaps.

It is told through a series of flashbacks. Denzel meets with Reeve at a bar
(hilariously, Johnny's new bar "Johnny's Heaven") and tells him the story of how
he ended up in the situation that kicks off the movie. The story keeps jumping
to the present so that Reeve and Denzel can add commentary, but it is 90%
focused on Denzel's actual story.

|| It turns out, he was an upper Midgar kid who lost his family to the Sector 7
collapse. A significant chunk of the story is about that trauma and how he felt
in those early days. He ends up staying with a random woman who protects him
(who Reeve reveals was his mother, plot twist!) until he loses her too. Then he
joins a gang of kids. Loses them too. Denzel keeps losing everyone, and is on
the brink of death when Cloud finds him randomly at the church in the slums.
Importantly, it is at the moment of peak despair where he catches Geostigma. ||

There are several things I like here, and I think this is one of the best
stories in the book. It shows us a side of several main FFVII story events that
we've never seen before, and the real human cost that was involved. It's also a
pretty decent, emotional story about loss and found family.

However, I still don't really love Denzel, and I find the Compilation's
obsession with him to be very strange. It feels like they were trying to build
up to Denzel being a protagonist in some future work, but it never happened.
Maybe it would have been worth it in that case, but in hindsight it feels like
it would have been better to focus on characters we already knew instead of
inventing a new guy just for this one purpose.

Furthering the obsession with Denzel, this story was turned into an animated OVA
that released as a bonus feature on the _Advent Children Complete_ Blu-ray. None
of the other stories got this treatment. I watched it. It was... fine. There
were a few interesting bits of spectacle in a few places, but I don't think the
emotional core of this story was improved by the adaptation to a new medium.
Though I suppose "who the hell is Denzel?" is _still_ a common enough question
for audiences watching _Advent Children Complete_ that they felt the need to
include this animated feature to explain it, since it's more likely those
audiences would watch a brief video than read a book. Would have been even
better if an explanation was not needed in the first place.

## Episode: Tifa

In _Advent Children_, Cloud and Tifa are going through... something. Their
relationship is strained. Cloud seems emotionally unavailable and hasn't been
back home in a long time. I think this is easy enough to pick up from context
clues, but this story exists to explain it again anyways. It's one of the more
dull stories in the book, seeming to only exist out of obligation to
thoroughness than for any actual expansion or exploration of the world or its
characters.

Cloud has a bike in the movie? We've gotta explain that! The explanation is...
he found a bike one day. Yeah. Cloud runs a delivery service in the movie! That,
too, must be explained: One day he got the idea to start a delivery service.
That's it. This entire story is just that.

## Episode: Barret

This is where they made Barret a centrist. It is by far the worst story in the
book, because it is complete character arc assassination.

It starts in a relatively reasonable place (straining a bit). Now that the main
fight is over, Barret wants to get rid of his gun arm. He wants to put down his
weapons and start a life dedicated to peace. So he begins working to pay for a
more normal prosthetic replacement.

There are a few problems. First, Barret sees how hard life is now that Mako is
gone. Workers are back in coal mines, and he sweats out long rides across the
continent in slow, hot, boiler-powered trucks. Travel is slow, because airships
use Mako (which I _think_ is a retcon that unnecessarily muddies the role of the
Highwind in the original). Slow, hard travel means even saving lives is harder
and less likely to succeed. Second, though the biggest threats are gone, there
are still monsters in the world. Nature is a dangerous place, even if we seek to
protect it. Ironically, all of Barret's work to secure an un-weaponized arm
relies heavily on his use of it to protect others.

What is a firebrand like Barret to do in these situations? Blaze a new path and
be a relentless champion of his cause? No. Become a centrist. Barret Both Sides
the situation and decides that Mako is good, actually. Then he gets into the oil
drilling business. Yeah. You remember how Mako was an analogue for oil in the
original game, and there was this whole environmentalist theme throughout? These
writers don't!

This whole journey is heavily undermined if you've already watched the movie,
because you'd know that Barret _does_ get it both ways. He gains a prosthetic
hand that can transform into a gun as needed.

None of this story explains why Barret made the dumbest choice in the entirety
of _Advent Children_: leaving Marlene. He just... does.

There are no redeeming parts of this story.

## Episode: Nanaki

The best part of this story is that it actually uses the name Nanaki.

Otherwise it's not great. Nanaki experiences some darkness take hold in his
heart. It's fear of the future. I don't know why this is focused on _now_ as if
it is a new thing, when this was already a part of his character in the
original, but sure. New fear of the future unlocked. Nanaki names this fear
"Gilligan" for reasons beyond my comprehension, and refers to it like another
character the whole time. It's maddening and I do not get it.

The rest of the story covers Nanaki returning to nature for a while. He refuses
to talk, and explores his wild side. But he finds that his increased
intelligence relative to other beasts makes it hard for him to fit into that
world. On the other hand, he gets hunted by humans who clearly view him as a
beast. He seemingly can't win, except among friends.

The second best thing about this story is the bonding Nanaki and Vincent do over
their long life spans. Though others may pass around them, they'll have each
other long term, and that brings them peace.

## Episode: Yuffie

Yuffie's story is a bit strangely placed, because it is spoiled by the Nanaki
story right before it. All these stories intersect and interact, but the
interaction between these two stories felt so forced and arbitrary that I
question why it was included. It only served to spoil this story for the sake of
an unnecessary cameo, in a book already featuring that character.

This story focuses less on Yuffie and more on Geostigma. To be fair, I support
that choice. Not because I dislike Yuffie, but because I think the ways they
attempted to evolve Yuffie's story here fell flat. On the other hand, Geostigma
is so confusing and unexplained in the movie that any additional details on it
are welcome.

The two main things we learn are that it is "in the water" (sorta) and also it
is triggered by despair. This fits well with _Advent Children's_ concept that
water and the Lifestream are linked. In the story, this leads to the hilarious
creation of a big sign in Wutai saying "Boil all drinking water. Don't think
about dying." First part should already be common knowledge. Second part just
makes me think about dying more. But Yuffie continues to be our lovable little
dumbass.

## Episode: Shinra

This one gets into spoilers for the original game: || In the game, Rufus dies.
He is killed by Diamond Weapon just after it dies to the Sister Ray. However, he
is mysteriously alive in _Advent Children_, with no explanation. This story
gives that explanation, as well as a lot more that happened to Rufus between the
game and the movie. ||

|| He escaped because his father made an escape route at his request, labeled
"L" for "Loser" because only losers need escape routes, apparently. However, not
only was this escape hatch poorly made (possibly intentionally), the building
containing it was being destroyed in the process. So Rufus ended up breaking
several bones including ribs, leaving him with lots of pain and limited
mobility. ||

|| Then we go beyond the "how did he survive" explanation and into Rufus being
kidnapped, tortured, and nearly drowned while the basement he was kept in
flooded and he had to tread water while tied up and still having broken bones in
order to survive. Meanwhile other captives were screaming and drowning all
around him. This section is just Rufus suffering porn. I assume they really
wanted to make Rufus a more sympathetic figure in the Compilation. Corporate
apologia, yay! This is where Rufus contracts Geostigma. What do you know,
despair and water. That thread is consistent throughout the book. ||

This chapter did give us some good Turks content, at least.

## Lifestream: Black and White

Throughout the book, woven between the other stories are individual chapters of
"Lifestream: White" and "Lifestream: Black." As the names suggest, these stories
mirror each other. Both contain tons of spoilers for the base game, but somehow
not really? They are constructed in an interesting way. No names are used, only
pronouns and context clues. But when you put all the clues together, they reveal
more about the world of FFVII than any other story. I enjoyed the puzzle, and
the reward.

|| "Lifestream: Black" covers Sephiroth's experience after the events of the
game. He has dissolved into the lifestream. He is _obsessed_ with Cloud, to the
point that it feels like Cloud is the object of his next madness after his life
has been a string of different obsessive madnesses and delusions. He's
essentially too mad to pass away, and his spirit perseveres in the lifestream.
He enacts a plan to return to the world and once again take over the Planet. He
begins spreading despair through the Lifestream, causing Geostigma. He also
creates his avatars, the Advent Children, who will bring about his return. ||

|| "Lifestream: White" tells Aerith's story in the Lifestream. Largely, it
covers her constant battle with Sephiroth's spirit as she resisted his influence
and healed the darkness he was spreading. She realized that she could win with
her old friends' help. Though this made her sad because her friends deserved
peace and she didn't want to see them hurt again. She resolved to involve them
and help them for the sake of the Planet, and all the people on it. She chose
this path rather than creating avatars of her own, believing their bonds would
be stronger than her power alone. ||

## Overall

As is probably the nature of collections of short stories like this, it was a
mixed bag. There are some interesting bits of information in here, and some nice
bits of storytelling occasionally. But most of it is filler and detailed
explanations for things that shouldn't have needed explanations. Unfortunately,
there's also some legitimately bad writing in here too, and stuff that actively
hurts my enjoyment of the original by this being canon.

# Advent Children Complete

You may be thinking, "didn't they already review this movie?" and your
recollection is correct. I didn't originally want to split things up like this.
I _wanted_ to review the most updated version of the movie, just like how I've
played the remasters of each game where available. However, I learned **only
after writing my review** that the copy I watched was _labeled Advent Children
Complete_ but wasn't the complete edition at all. It was uploaded under the
wrong title. So I never watched the complete edition. Thanks Amazon. Oh well, at
least this gave me the opportunity to review the movie before and after these
changes, which was legitimately interesting thanks to changes in the Compilation
over time. But the cost was watching this movie again. Not sure that was worth
it.

There are lots of really tiny changes. Characters are bloodier and dirtier. The
names of some places got re-localized to read better. Some facial expressions
were altered to better fit the context. Stuff like that.

The larger changes are whole new scenes. Over 20 minutes of content was added to
the movie. Some of it is rather silly: The opening already had a "500 years ago"
followed by a "2 weeks later," but now it jumps around a bit more, like "500
years ago, 3 weeks later, 2 weeks ago, 2 weeks later" and so on. It's almost a
parody of itself. Most of the rest of the new scenes were added to provide more
context for certain characters, including some backstory for Denzel as mentioned
above. Finally, just about every action sequence has been extended
significantly, even the ones that were mostly included for comedic effect.

What I find most interesting are the changes that were made to catch the movie
up on the trajectory of the compilation, and to alter that trajectory.

Some of those changes had to do with character designs and motifs. Cait Sith did
not have his moogle in the early Compilation entries. It is back in this
edition. This makes me wonder if there was a failed attempt to redesign his
character, but they went back to the original. Sephiroth is shown with that
black wing **constantly** in this version of the movie, when he was only shown
that way for one brief scene in the original. Zack wears his Crisis Core outfit.
These are all used by the Remake series.

A few other continuity things are changed to match how they were portrayed in
the Compilation. Most notably were (big spoilers for the original game) ||
Zack's final moments with Cloud and generally all of Cloud's flashbacks of him.
These were replaced to match their Crisis Core counterparts. Though some are
remakes of Last Order scenes, with the retcons filed off. It's nice to see nods
to that project while correcting its issues, rather than simply ignoring it ever
happened. ||

Other changes are easier to miss but have much bigger implications. In the
original ending of the game || Midgar was shown 500 years in the future, with
nature having retaken it. There was originally supposed to be smoke rising from
the ruins, indicating humans were still there all those years later, but living
in harmony with nature this time. However it was accidentally left out of the
final release of the game. This made the ending of the game ambiguous, as it was
never confirmed whether or not humanity survived. That ambiguity made the ending
so much stronger, but it was a happy accident. The original _Advent Children_
added this smoke back into the relevant scene, confirming the unrealized
intention to indicate that humanity would survive. _Advent Children Complete_
once again removed that smoke, and this time it could not have been a mistake. I
think that the direct removal of this smoke does not indicate ambiguity, but an
ending where humanity is wiped out. ||

I'm going to tie all that to the Remake series, since I played the first game
six years ago and I vaguely recall some details that are relevant here. So the
following contains spoilers only for that game: || In it, we are shown a vision
of the _Advent Children Complete_ version of that scene, and it is explicitly
referred to as "a glimpse of tomorrow if we fail here today." Additionally,
Sephiroth has the black wing this movie doubled down on, and the party fights
him in a scene that looks strikingly like an action sequence from the movie. ||
The Remake series was already being planned when this movie was being made. I
think these changes were all made specifically to tie into the eventual Remake
series and it's continuity. It is the bridge between the Compilation and the
Remake.

That brings me back to some movie changes, and some wild theories: || Not only
does Sephiroth summon that black wing earlier in the fight this time around, he
seems to have knowledge of Cloud's moves a few times. That's why we get some
more combat scenes---Sephiroth keeps countering Cloud's attacks from the first
movie, leading us into new situations. This culminates in Sephiroth countering
Cloud's Level 4 Omnislash Limit Break, stabbing Cloud like he did in the
original game, and then performing his own dark and brutal version of Omnislash
on Cloud. Eventually, Cloud finishes the fight with an all-new Level 5
Omnislash, and the rest of the movie plays out like normal from then on. ||

|| Here's what I think: The multiverse/time-travel/fate stuff from FFVII Remake
actually started all the way back in _Advent Children Complete_. It even has
wisps of dark lifestream under Sephiroth's control that look a lot like the dark
whispers of Remake. Sephiroth seems to be stronger, earlier, and knows what
Cloud is going to do ahead of time. This pushes Cloud to new heights to defeat
him, but Cloud also seems mysteriously stronger from the start. It's like...
they've done this before. It's like this is a loop, with Sephiroth trying again
and again, entering the timeline earlier and earlier, stronger each time. It
would also track that humanity's future fate could be changing in each timeline
as Sephiroth meddles further and further back. So is _Advent Children_ one
timeline, and _Advent Children Complete_ another, later loop? If we extrapolate
that enough cycles forward, Sephiroth would start appearing in this manner
during the events of the original game---and he does, in Remake. ||

Wrapping up the movie discussion---should you watch it? I think if you enjoy the
hell out of the Remake series, yes. It's still the best, most important, and
most accessible part of the Compilation. However, as with everything related to
the Remake project, it's tricky. Knowing this stuff will likely enhance your
enjoyment of the Remake series, but it also contains spoilers for the original
game (and therefore spoilers for the Remake series). So there's no right answer.
My ultimate recommendation is to play the original, watch this, and then play
the Remake series. But if you're already deep in the Remake series or have no
intention of playing the original, hold off on this. That said, it's still not a
fantastic movie. So save it for a night when you just want to throw on something
fun and light and not care for a bit. Maybe laugh at the combat scenes a bit,
that kind of stuff. Or just skip it. It is most valuable as a piece of history
leading up to, and influencing the creation of, the Remake series.

# The Kids Are Alright: A Turks Side Story

Another _Advent Children_ prequel book, woo!

I thought this was fine. It's pretty average young adult thriller stuff. A lot
of the community seems to love this one, and the creators clearly do as well
because several references are made to it in the Remake series. I just don't see
it. Then again I'm not much of a novel reader in general, and this genre isn't
for me. Further, Kazushige Nojima is pretty hit-or-miss for me. So it was always
going to be a hard sell. Maybe my rating of "just fine" under those
circumstances means it's actually pretty good.

The story follows Evan Townshend and his friends as they try to survive after
the events of the original game. They are the kids in the title. As the other
part of the title implies, this focuses heavily on the Turks as well. The reason
these two stories intertwine is that || Evan is revealed to be Rufus Shinra's
secret half brother. ||

This is less of an expansion of the world of FFVII and more of "another story in
that world." It barely touches on existing characters (outside of the Turks),
and when it does it feels unnecessary and forced. Even the Turks themselves are
hardly developed by this story. It's just another excuse to see them in action.
The exception is Elena. Her personality is massively changed from earlier works.
She is now extremely tough and almost **hungry** for violence. She's been
transformed into a callous "shoot first, questions later" action movie
anti-hero.

The main place this story expands upon the world of FFVII is by exploring its
darker, grittier side. This isn't a story about heroes and epic quests and
realm-shaking stakes and light versus darkness. It's a story about humans and
the often messed up stuff they do to each other, rough circumstances, and
looking out for each other through it all. We get to see what Don Corneo is up
to these days, for example, and he's an ever more sick and twisted bastard than
before. In general, this book takes the dark elements of the original that were
made light, and sheds that protective layer of comedy to show the dirt
underneath.

On top of it all, this is still a prequel to _Advent Children_. It sets the
movie up in a few places, though I would argue those elements of the story felt
forced and didn't actually add much to this book or the movie. Once again, the
Compilation's prequels feel the need to offer explicit explanations for things
that were best left unexplained and could be easily understood already through
context. || It turns out that the three avatars of Sephiroth got their names and
appearances because they first encountered Kyrie, and shaped themselves off of
her memories in true Jenova fashion. That event happens in this book. The black
smoky substance bleeds into the water just like it did in the movie, and an
avatar is born. It becomes Kadaj, and immediately starts asking all the same
things Kadaj does in the movie, "where's Mother?" and so on. It's a nice retcon
that the avatars have names due to Jenova-like powers---but if they have
Jenova-like powers, it would have been way cooler and creepier for them to use
those in the movies and take on the names and faces of whoever they were talking
to at the time, remaining nameless themselves. This book is also where Rufus and
the Turks begin their search for parts of Jenova in the Northern Crater, which
leads directly into the start of the movie. But did that really need an
explanation? Finally, this book includes stuff from _On the Way to a Smile_ by
having Aerith in the Lifestream rescue Even and Kyrie by saving them from the
Lifestream and depositing them in a spring of water. This also mirrors how Cloud
is saved in the movie. ||

I think this is more worth reading than _On the Way to a Smile_ if you're
**super** into the world of FFVII and need more of it, no matter what. But it's
pretty easily skippable. I don't think it changes my opinions or enhances my
experience of any other part of the series, but it was decent. I'll enjoy
pointing at the screen when I recognize characters from this one in the Remake
games, but it's nothing more than a footnote in the series overall.

# Ever Crisis: The First Soldier

This is a weird one to cover, because it's kinda two games, and kinda not? The
First Solider was a mobile phone Fortnite clone that died about a year after it
launched. Well, the player base died a few weeks after it launched. The servers
were just left uselessly online for a year after that. It had no real story to
it except "this is Shinra's VR training program." Ever Crisis is a gacha game
that, at the time of writing, will go offline in two weeks. It has no story of
its own, either. Instead, it recaps the stories of all other Compilation
entries, from the original game to Before Crisis to Dirge of Cerberus and
beyond. Everything. Even The First Soldier! You may be asking, how does it recap
the story of a game that didn't have a story? It didn't. It borrowed the name
and made up a new story. So this is the closest thing to a main story that
_both_ games got, and they share it.

The other odd thing about covering this now is that it _technically_ came out
after the first Remake game. So in theory, I should save it to cover after that.
However, I think it fits better here. It is, in spirit, Compilation content, not
Remake content, especially since the rest of Ever Crisis is Compilation content.
It doesn't contain anything that requires the Remake story to properly
experience or talk about. If you told me it came out before the Remake was even
announced, I'd believe it. Also it would be too small to cover on its own.

I'm not going to cover the gameplay for this game, because I didn't play it.
Instead I watched someone else play it with commentary and skipped gameplay
segments so I could experience the story like something between a movie and an
audiobook. But, in brief: It's a pretty standard gacha system with an ATB combat
system similar to the original game but much more mobile-game-ified.

This story had three separate episodes, which had very different focuses.

## Episode I

This episode stars Glenn, Matt, and Lucia. They are members of the original "P0"
prototype SOLDIERs, which were entirely normal people selected for their natural
capabilities and given the best equipment and training. Sort of like a more
militarized version of the Turks. Mako was not given to this line of SOLDIERs.

They are on a mission to scout and clear a location for a new Mako reactor, but
the chosen site is home to an ancient civilization. Not only does the wilderness
of the location prove to be more than they can deal with, they also quickly
became disillusioned with Shinra's mission after seeing the people they were
sent to kill live such normal lives. When they hesitate, a young Sephiroth
appears and takes command.

Young Sephiroth is the most interesting part of this episode, and the true main
character, though not quite the protagonist. He falls short of the iconic look
we're all familiar with. He's not as tall or imposing. His hair is silver, but
short. Even his sword is smaller. He's also uncertain and socially awkward.
Having only really known Hojo and being constantly sent on solo missions around
the world, he's never had time to stop and make friends. So our main cast of
three become his first friends.

We don't learn too many details about Sephiroth we didn't already know. The
backstory elements opened up here are largely repeated from the original story,
just shown off in a game instead of only mentioned as a background detail.
However, we get to see that he wasn't always the dark, imposing, insane, cold
monster he's known as today. He was once a kid.

The moment of contrast comes later. || At the end of the episode, Sephiroth is
face to face with the last remaining member of this ancient civilization that
worships and tends to the Lifestream, a child his age named Rosen, who has had
the world placed on his shoulders just like Sephiroth. In a way, they can see
eye to eye. The child begs Sephiroth to kill him so he can return to his family.
Sephiroth stabs him through the chest with no hesitation. I think parallels are
intentionally drawn here with Aerith. It is at that moment Sephiroth is revealed
to be no ordinary child. Nature or nurture, who can say? Glenn and the others
watched this happen from nearby, as they screamed at Sephiroth to stop. After
witnessing all these things, they defect from Shinra, and Sephiroth feels
abandoned by those closest to him, once more. ||

## Episode II

This episode takes place later, after the SOLDIERs from Crisis Core started
getting activated. In fact, it stars Angeal, similarly young like Sephiroth.
This story leans on the supernatural/spooky side, including some weird
Jenova-style manipulative magic. It is the story of how Sephiroth got both his
iconic sword, the Masamune, and finally || became as fully connected to Jenova
|| as he is in the original game.

Sephiroth and Angeal head to a cursed area in Wutai during the Wutai conflict.
People they meet there have illusions projected over them to look like people
from the past of whoever they are talking to, very Jenova style. The cursed
characters are unaware of this. Unsettling. Spirits continue to torment the
party psychologically, including manipulative visions of alternative versions of
events they've experienced. To break free, they each have to accept painful and
traumatic parts of their pasts. The spirits seem to latch on to Sephiroth in
particular, forcing him to choose who lives and dies, and trying to lure him
into dreams that would trap him in eternal slumber. All the while, Sephiroth
feels instinctually attracted to a cursed sword at the center of the tragic
story of this cursed place, as if it was always meant for him. When he finally
claims it and masters it, he gains the ability to summon and dismiss it at will.

Later on || it is revealed that the spirits in that place and the curse put upon
it were all related to Jenova's spiritual energy bubbling up from the
Lifestream. The blade was forged of Jenova's will, and was indeed always meant
for Sephiroth. This was the first time they truly reunited and Sephiroth began
his path of reunion with Jenova. ||

## Episode - Three Ex-SOLDIERs

Our three heroes from the first episode have formed the Shinra Resistance
Committee. It seems like they've been through some crazy stuff. They bear tons
of scars and wear much scrappier, edgier outfits. They overthrow the
Shinra-installed Wutai government and establish the provisional Wutai
government. || It turns out that everything was Shinra manipulation from the
start. They were led by Rufus in his attempt to overthrow his father. When his
father died at the start of the original game, Rufus no longer needed Glenn and
the others, and so betrayed them. He shoots Glenn, but Glenn's fate is left
uncertain as he drifts in the Lifestream and hears a voice heavily implied to be
Sephiroth, to whom Glenn says he'll do anything to see his work done. ||

These events weave into the narrative of Crisis Core and the start of FFVII
nicely. I actually quite liked this episode for finding out how several
background elements connected together. It was short, but decent. Also I
appreciate the portrayal of Rufus as the evil bastard he is, rather than the
corporate apologia of _Advent Children_ and its related media.

# Final Thoughts

That brings me to the end of the Compilation content. It was interesting to
experience, and while I don't recommend anyone else go as deep as I did, I look
forward to seeing all the references in the Remake series and coming up with my
own theories as I go. I don't think there were any parts of the Compilation that
I overall loved. Some I enjoyed at least somewhat. Most were fine at best. The
most enjoyable part was not the content itself, but analyzing and comparing the
content over time to see the history of the series' development unfold.

There were at first, three separate threads. There was _Advent Children_, Dirge
of Cerberus, and Crisis Core. All three had slightly different elements, but
each fit into the greater set of Compilation motifs. For a while, _Advent
Children_ got the most love, but eventually all three of these branches were
intertwined into one---and the Remake project is the ultimate unification and
reforging of all Compilation elements, good or bad. In a way, that's similar to
Lightning Returns, and that's one of the reasons that is my favorite Final
Fantasy game. Perhaps it should come as no surprise that they were made by
largely the same teams.

Next time, The Unknown Journey Will Begin. I look forward to it.
