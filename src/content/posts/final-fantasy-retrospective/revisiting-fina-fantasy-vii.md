A year and a half ago, I wrote that Final Fantasy VII was "A masterpiece for
everyone but me, apparently." I didn't know how wrong I was at the time. Now, I
finally get it.

There are plenty of things I said in that original review that remain true. It
took _a lot_ to get this game through my skull. Over many years, it took no less
than four attempts at starting the game before I finally finished it. Even on
that final, complete playthrough, I didn't much enjoy it. I was simply motivated
by the goal I set myself, to complete this review series. Yet, through all those
years, even before I completed the game, Final Fantasy VII stuck with me.

In my own creative work---the graveyard of unfinished projects, ideas kicking
around in my head and only rarely, partially written down---I was often inspired
by Final Fantasy VII. Something about this world and these characters resonated
with me, even if I hadn't yet seen the whole picture.

When I finally saw that full picture, I didn't get it.

But I think, some part of me did. I sometimes found myself crying listening to
this soundtrack, without understanding why. There was something inside me that I
wasn't ready to face yet. Something painful to acknowledge. Multiple somethings.
Probably more than I yet know.

Shortly after finishing FFVII for the first time, I removed the Compilation of
FFVII entries from my "plan to play" list for this retrospective. I figured, if
I didn't like FFVII, why would I spend more time and effort on its derivatives?
Over time, those feelings softened, and eventually were replaced with
enthusiasm. At first, I added the most accessible entries. Then I added them
all, even the books, short stories, OVAs, and dead games. Even with all of that
thoroughness, I could have chosen to skip over FFVII itself, since I technically
already have a review for it. I chose not to. After making that choice, I
started looking forward to it.

What changed?

Well, probably the earliest thing I should get out of the way is that this time
I modded the game. The fan community around Final Fantasy VII has created some
truly incredible mods for it. While there are some crazy remaster-type mods out
there, that wasn't the experience I wanted to have this time. I wanted to
maintain the spirit of the original while using surgical precision to alter the
few specific stylistic elements that I didn't like. The two main issues for me
were the original localization and the character models. I decided to use the
excellent Shinra Archaeology Cut translation and NiNo models. These were massive
improvements to my enjoyment of the game. I think I was so distracted by these
issues during my previous playthroughs that I found it hard to focus on the
story and take it seriously. This time, I was eager to consume it all.

I use the word "issues" but I should probably be clear that I only mean they
were issues for me. Potentially, skill issues on my part for being unable to
focus on the story with these character models and the inconsistent tone of the
localization. I know some fans love those originals, and I don't think they're
wrong! They just weren't my cup of tea. I'm glad these options are out there for
people like me, because they were instrumental in my transformation into a
diehard fan of this game.

The other critical thing that changed, was me! I think some distance from this
game gave its ideas time to percolate in my head, and I also think it is one of
those games with a **way** stronger second playthrough thanks to that effect.
Slightly more importantly, I've gotten better at this narrative analysis stuff
in the last year. Where I last saw a disconnected mess, I now see all the
brilliant connections. Plus I'm a bit more willing to look over some flaws here
and there to appreciate the good stuff where it shines, when I used to latch
onto the flaws most of all. Most important of all, I've gone through some
serious loss since I last played this. Now that I've finally finished processing
it all, I understand the message.

This is not going to be a super in-depth review. Maybe not _really_ a review at
all, certainly not the kind of reviews I've been writing recently.
[I've covered the game once before.](/posts/final-fantasy-vii/) Even if I
disagree with a lot of what I said in my initial take, there's plenty I still
agree with. Instead, I'm just going to talk about the things that stuck out to
me this time around, and why this game has become easily one of my top three
Final Fantasy games.

I'm going to focus mostly on the story, as that's what I failed to get the first
time around. Since the Final Fantasy VII Remake series is still ongoing, I will
mark spoilers based on which part of that series they spoil, by including
(Remake), (Rebirth), or (Revelation) before the spoiler text. I have yet to play
Rebirth, and Revelation is not yet out, so these are best guesses, but I'm
leaning very heavily on the safe side.

# The Story

As I played through the game this time, I live-blogged my experience to some
friends on discord. I think I'll do that for at least some of my retrospective
reviews in the future. It helped me take much better notes. I announce that here
to describe how I wrote this section: I essentially went through my live notes
and condensed the stuff that hit me hardest. I normally don't love to do recaps
like this in my reviews these days, but this isn't really a review. So I'll do
it just this once.

## Midgar

I took _a lot_ of notes on Midgar this time around. I'm going to do my best to
boil them down to something digestible here, but many things will be left on the
cutting room floor. This section of the game is **so packed** with details that
I stumbled on something meaningful everywhere I looked. Most of those things I
was only able to pick up on after completing the game once. I can't cover all of
them, but I'll cover some.

First off, I have to say that this is very likely my favorite Final Fantasy
intro. The opening hours of this game are flawless. They sell the world so hard,
set up so much foreshadowing and introduce the themes of the story, all without
giving anything away too much. It's masterful.

(Remake) || The first interaction with Aerith is delightfully surreal. Already
in the intro, we saw her stand out among the city of neon and smoke around her,
but now she stands out even more. The party is literally fleeing an act of
violence they caused, but Cloud stops for a quiet moment with this girl selling
flowers. In this concrete hellscape with not a single leaf in sight, she has
brought a bit of nature to sell, and she isn't charging much! There's some
wonderful contrast with how Cloud asks for thousands of Gil to commit violence,
but Aerith sells a smile and a refreshing bit of beautiful nature for a single
Gil. ||

(Remake) || The _proper_ introduction to Aerith comes later, when Cloud falls
into the slums below Midgar. He somehow miraculously survives this fall, and
finds himself not only in a church, but in a small patch of flowers living in
Midgar. Three miracles! Who else could be there but Aerith? The game sets her up
as someone special, almost holy, right off the bat. She is full of joy and
whimsy, contrasting every other personality we've come across so far just as
much as her flower-filled church contrasts with the rest of Midgar. ||

(Remake) || The way the mystery of Sephiroth builds up is literally perfect. We
first hear of him in name only, mentioned as Cloud's hero and reason for joining
SOLDIER. Then, we see in a flashback Tifa screaming that she hates SOLDIER
_because Sephiroth killed her dad._ Then president Shinra can't stop talking
about how strong Sephiroth is when he shows up to stop the party. For this
entire first act of the game, we never actually _see_ Sephiroth. He's
continually built up as a terrifying legend, and his whole deal is left as a
mystery. It's some great horror writing, leaving the monster out of the
audience's perception until a later grand reveal. ||

(Remake) || This builds up until we get to the point of transition between acts
of the story: the Shinra building. After a long and arduous climb through the
building, the party is captured. They are set free under mysterious
circumstances, and find the nearby area filled with blood, corpses, and claw
marks. The trail leads from the Jenova containment unit upstairs, where we find
Sephiroth's sword planted in president Shinra's back. ||

(Revelation) || This is an excellent, well disguised hint for what we learn
later: We've never seen Sephiroth or his work at all, not even here. Those with
Jenova cells can be overtaken by Sephiroth's will, granting them his appearance
and power. He's been doing this across the entire world to manipulate events in
his favor, including the path of the party. ||

This section of the game is perfectly paced from start to finish.

## The Journey

As the characters travel the world, they also go on a journey of self discovery.
The party visits each of their home towns, where we learn about them and they
learn about themselves. The thematic parallels between these stories are so
perfectly executed.

(Rebirth) || The transition from Midgar into the greater world is spectacular.
Many JRPGs have an Airship Moment, But rarely is there a World Map Moment like
this. The world opens up to you, and Midgar seems suddenly so small. At the same
time, more about the world is revealed, when we are finally shown Sephiroth for
the first time as we learn Cloud's backstory, and something is very clearly
wrong here. Really, we learn more about Sephiroth than Cloud. ||

(Revelation) || It is fitting that not only is this the first place we visit,
but it's also nobody's home town. Cloud's story is fake. This is not a home
town, so we don't get a real moment of self discovery. The next few places we
visit are also not home towns, and are primarily further Cloud's self-deception.
Cloud must literally _pretend_ to be a Shinra soldier to get through Junon, and
the city does little except isolate him and drive him to chase Sephiroth more.
In Costa del Sol, Hojo encourages Cloud to follow the subconscious messages Hojo
realizes Cloud has been receiving, pulling him towards the Reunion. Even the
Gold Saucer has a fake out moment making us doubt Barret before we get his real
backstory in Corel. ||

(Rebirth) || Barret's backstory is my favorite. It is revealed that he took up
the environmental activist and anti-Shinra mask to hide the pain of his
self-hatred for feeling responsible for his home town being destroyed.
Obviously, Shinra _is_ evil, and Barret's goal of saving the Planet _is_ noble,
but that's not his real motivation deep down. Also, the way he goes about his
activism is the same path that led to his home town being destroyed. We get to
see Barret confront his shadow through Dyne, seeing the dark reflection of what
he bears deep inside and what he could have easily become. He is forced to
reconcile with the blood he has on his hands, and begins to understand his true
reason for fighting. ||

(Rebirth) || Cosmo Canyon is a close second place behind Corel. I'm not a huge
fan of Nanaki's character design, but I love his story. Nanaki hid his identity
and pretended to be tough and wise because he was ashamed of a part of himself
and where he came from. He thought his father was shameful, and so thought he
inherited some of that shame. This made him want to escape his identity.
Crucially, he was wrong. He was missing information. When he learned it, he
became proud of his his father and subsequently his own identity. This part also
has a huge impact on the overarching story, beyond just Nanaki's arc. There's a
critical lore dump that covers some of the most important concepts in this
world, and there are tons of themes of acceptance here---both acceptance of ones
own identity and acceptance of death as a natural part of life. ||

I don't intend to recap everything that happens in the journey, so I'll skip
ahead a bit: the most important landmark is (Rebirth) || Aerith's death. It is
so sudden, so devastating, and so disruptive that it is no surprise that it has
become one of the most memorable scenes in gaming history. I'm truncating this
_extremely heavily._ I took more notes here than almost anywhere else. Not
because of the crazy lore or symbolism or whatever, but just what I was feeling
in each moment. It's so masterfully executed. Cloud's insecurity that he can't
save anyone is dredged back up stronger than ever, paired with an uncertainty
that he is even in control of his own actions, a danger to everyone around him.
||

## The Struggle

(Revelation) || In this unsteady state, Sephiroth strikes---not at Cloud's
physical heart, but his emotional one. Through lies laced with truth and
manipulation of Tifa through the secrets she has kept, Sephiroth destroys
Cloud's sense of self. His previous identity _was_ false, something he came up
with as a defense mechanism, but Sephiroth shows him another falsehood to
replace it with. Desperate for a new identity, Cloud takes it. He even begs Hojo
for an experiment number to refer to himself as, believing his old name no
longer belongs to him. He feels so small and insignificant that even something
as dehumanizing as an experiment number feels like an upgrade to him. ||

(Revelation) || As Cloud's identity is shattered, the world approaches its doom.
Terrifying monsters roam the lands leaving nothing but ruins. A meteor hangs in
the sky, soon to destroy all life. Party members have been captured and are
sentenced to public execution. These are the end times. All the while, Cloud is
in a coma. Earlier, Aerith's death was brilliantly represented mechanically by
the loss of your party's only healer, making every combat feel like something
was missing. Now, the party must also operate without Cloud, making one more
thing feel missing. Though he is not dead, his identity is gone. Nobody can
quite fill the same role, even after multiple attempts at nominating a new
leader. ||

(Revelation) || Everything changes during easily my favorite sequence in the
game and likely one of the best sequences in the entire series: Tifa and Cloud
in the lifestream, untangling Cloud's mind. This is a radical expression of
vulnerability from Cloud, and care from Tifa. They are going through all of his
most emotionally charged memories, in a way where nothing can be kept hidden and
all is exposed. There may be nothing more intimate than this fantasy scenario,
and Tifa is so patient and gentle, laser focused on giving Cloud the help he
needs without judgement. She unravels Cloud's insecurities and leads him to true
self acceptance, represented by all the fragmented parts of his mind willingly
standing together and merging. However, it wasn't something she could force him
to do, he also had to participate and _want_ to be whole. Crucially, none of
this process **rejects** what Cloud has become. The persona he has worn since
the start of the game _is_ part of him. It's just no longer the _only_ part of
him. Now his past and all the pain _and_ joy within is part of him too. His
found family welcome him once again with a cheer and open arms. I cried more
here than during any other part. ||

## Forge Ahead

(Revelation) || The work is not yet done, but it is no longer such a struggle.
The party is reunited and things are getting easier, little by little. The party
faces some of the most terrifying threats plaguing the planet, and prepares for
the decisive battle. Importantly, they discover that Aerith left them hope: the
white materia, Holy, and the power to stop the meteor. ||

(Revelation) || Cloud gives a very passionate speech to the party about how they
all need to find their individual reasons to fight. They are not just
individuals, nor just a collective, but a collective of individuals. A goal such
as saving the world is obviously good, but there's no personal weight behind it.
It is too impersonal, too abstract and distant. It leaves room for doubt to
creep in. So, Cloud sends each party member into the world to find their
personal reason to fight. If they can't find anything, they should not return.
Barret realizes that his true reason to fight is for Marlene, for example. One
by one they all find something like that and return, uniting their individual
purposes into one common goal. ||

(Revelation) || The final fight with Sephiroth is brutal and an absolutely
terrifying spectacle. It's amazing. This is one of those fights that uses _all_
of your party members, regardless of the normal party size, so that it truly
feels like everyone is united for this last stand. It's one of the best final
bosses in the series. ||

(Revelation) || But it isn't over yet. Though they cut Sephiroth from the heart
of the Planet, Cloud must cut Sephiroth from his own heart. In a sequence
mirroring that of descending into the Planet's wound to get to its center, Cloud
descends into his own mind until he reaches the darkest, innermost part, where
Sephiroth stands shirtless representing his final vulnerability. It is here that
Cloud's reforged identity must crush his childhood hero and adulthood villain.
It's no contest. Cloud has already proven with his friends that Sephiroth can
bleed, he is no longer untouchable and legendary in Cloud's mind. ||

(Revelation) || The final moments in the game are the Planet, mirroring Cloud's
journey back to him. The Planet must decide what its identity will be going
forward, now that it's scattered parts have united and cut Sephiroth out to give
it that chance. Would it choose to keep humanity as part of itself? This is the
ultimate question, which is left unanswered. The party accept this. They do not
attempt to sway the Planet. They await the Planet's judgement. The player is
left without closure for these characters. The game asks the player to accept
that, as death is a part of life, these characters too must end. But it's not as
bleak as it sounds. As everything fades away, we are once again shown Aerith
superimposed over the cosmos, bookending the game. She, once again, represents a
spark of hope. Though there is uncertainty, there is always hope. ||

# The Message

To me, this is a story about acceptance, or perhaps understanding, of two
incredibly important aspects of life. First, our identities are ours alone, and
together we form the beautiful collective tapestry of humanity. Second, losing
some people we are close to is an unfortunately normal part of life, but this is
something we can recover from.

I'll do my best to remain spoiler free in this section.

## Identity

Throughout this game, we are shown time and time again the dangers associated
with bearing an identity that doesn't fit or was based on a misconception. These
identities could be masks we wear to hide something deep within that we don't
want others to see or that we find uncomfortable to face ourselves. These could
be identities given to us by others that don't know us well enough or have
harmful ideas in their heads. They could even be identities we assign ourselves
because of our own misunderstandings. These identities are harmful to those who
bear them, and often to those around them as well.

However this does not at all mean that identities are somehow a harmful concept.
This game also shows the strength of finding and accepting ones own identity.
This cast is a strange group of misfits who are all incredibly different from
each other. Yet, it is the combination of their unique strengths that makes
their bonds and their capabilities so strong together. I think the limit break
system is an expression of each character's individual will and identity.

It also does not mean that external identities never have any positive value.
The key is volition and wholehearted acceptance of an identity, external or
internal. The problems arise when that identity is not wished for and is merely
accepted for the sake of another or to fit in to society or some other external
pressure. The rejection of all identities that do not spring from within is not
at all the point.

This is consistent throughout the whole game, and nearly every aspect of the
game has something to say on this topic. It's one of the most powerful messages
of the game, which spoke to me deeply. If you play this or the Remake series,
look for it.

## Loss

Much more famously, this game deals heavily with loss. It's not isolated to only
a few incidents, either. Every character in this party has lost something or
someone precious to them, and is still dealing with that pain every day. They
all deal with it differently, but it's always a defining moment in each
character's story.

The loss continues into the present day of the game, as well. That isn't
isolated to a small number of incidents either. These characters _continue_ to
deal with it. It never stops. It always hurts.

Yet, they carry on. They have each other. This found family cares for one
another and comforts one another. Together, they move past it. They heal.

We are also shown that the inability to move on causes great suffering. It is
considered one of the worst things that can happen. In fact, the ultimate evil
plan is to (Rebirth) || cut off the means of moving on for everyone. In so
doing, cut off the means of living as well. Death brings meaning to life, and
life brings meaning to death. ||

# Final Thoughts

**Rating: 9/10**  
_Playtime: 38 hours_

I love this game. The messages of acceptance of identity and the realities of
life are powerful and strike me deeply. Though these are hard, often dark topics
that can easily lead to despair and dread, the game delivers its ideas like a
warm hug and a hand on your shoulder. It is here to comfort you about these
things, not to challenge you about them. It is profoundly hopeful in a way that
few other games I've experienced are.

So... why not a 10/10? Mostly, I'm very strict about those. As much as I love
this game, I also think it has some serious flaws. I'm glad I found mods that
made the story accessible for me, but it would be better if those weren't
necessary. Though I appreciate the humor present throughout the game, there are
a few serious scenes that are ruined by poorly timed humor. I think Cait Sith
and Cid were handled extremely poorly and drag the game down. Finally I find the
combat and progression systems to be passable at best, and I care quite a lot
about those systems in my personal ratings and rankings.

Still, there's so much I enjoy about this game that I can't see myself rating it
anything lower than a 9/10. Those flaws are minor when compared to the things I
love and appreciate.

As I finish up writing this, I feel as though I've done a poor job, but I don't
think that's correct. This game means something to me that I think is very hard
to put into words, and some of its art is already expressed in the best possible
medium for it: a game. To translate it to raw text would always have to leave
something out. So, I think it's best not to try that hard at it. I'll accept
that it's good enough to indulge in my desire to gush about this game I love.
Like life, this one's about the journey, and stopping to smell the roses.
Instead of listening to me talk about it, go take that journey yourself, and
experience feelings beyond words.

I now look to my future playthrough of the Remake series with renewed
excitement. I already love this story so much, and so many of my issues with it
can be easily fixed by that project. It is more accessible by default. Some of
the dated ideas have been updated. There's more detail and more room to cram
love for this game into every nook and cranny. I'm going to immerse myself into
it and it will be good.

Here's to a journey of self discovery a future of relentless hope.
