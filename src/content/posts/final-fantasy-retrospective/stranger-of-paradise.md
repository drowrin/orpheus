The "guy who's here to kill chaos" has achieved meme status reaching far beyond
the Final Fantasy fan base. Beneath the layer of absurdity is not only a very
good game, but a surprisingly clever and sincere tribute.

That said, Stranger of Paradise never actually drops the absurdity. It's wild
and nonsensical all the way through. But there's something deeper than
that---something I can't describe without getting into heavy spoilers, which I
_will_ talk about near the end of this review. I highly recommend going into
this game blind if you are a Final Fantasy fan.

I am not at all surprised to find out this was written by Kazushige Nojima. I
haven't talked about him nearly as much as I should have in this series. He has
written for some of the most out-there stuff in this franchise, including more
abstract mainline entries like FFVIII, as well as several spinoffs. He also
wrote for the Kingdom Hearts series. He works very closely with Tetsuya Nomura,
to the degree that a large number of his particular narrative stylistic choices
often get misattributed to Nomura.

This was originally announced as a Nioh-like remake of Final Fantasy I. It is
that, but with a significant twist or two, or five. If all they needed for such
a project was to polish up that original story and flesh out the details, they
would not have brought Nojima onto the project. I'm sure there are some out
there who are disappointed that this is not a direct remake, but I think the
unique interpretation at the core of Stranger of Paradise made it far more
interesting, and far more sincere a tribute than it otherwise would have been...
just, not in the way anyone expected.

# The Review

Evaluating it purely on quality, I don't think Stranger of Paradise: Final
Fantasy Origin is an outstanding game. There are relatively few parts of it that
stand out over anything else in the series, and I encountered a number of minor
annoyances. Yet, I find myself overall very positive about the latest, strangest
spinoff in the franchise. It has heart, and I find that more important than any
measure of objective quality.

## Gameplay

I think gameplay will be the highlight for many, especially those who have
played fewer Final Fantasy games. Anyone who is a fan of the genre will find
some excellent content here, though their appreciation will likely scale based
on how familiar they are with the franchise.

This is a Team Ninja game, and you can _feel_ it. Loot explodes out of nearly
everything, to the point that I sometimes needed to clear out my 600-limit
equipment space multiple times per level. Each one of those pieces of gear have
a mind-numbingly long list of active and passive abilities, synergies, and
interactions with multiple other systems. I just hit auto-equip a few times per
level and ignored the rest, in the same way I play Nioh games, but there's some
real depth here for those who love to tinker with this stuff. There are
different stamina rules for different kinds of enemies, multiple resources to
track mid-combat, multiple stances on some weapons, 30 superfluous features I'm
probably forgetting, and an absolutely insane skill ceiling. This is Nioh in a
Final Fantasy skin.

By far the best part, and what makes this more enjoyable for me than any other
Team Ninja game I've played, is the job system. It actually might be... one of
the best job systems in the series? High praise, but I think it gets there. Each
job levels up individually, and has its own skill tree. On that tree are passive
buffs and active abilities that you can assign to different parts of your
combos. They are both thematic to the job they appear on, but apply to you
regardless of your currently equipped job. Jobs are grouped into rough
categories based on playstyle and expected stats. Playing as more mage-type jobs
will improve your mana economy and magic damage, but playing as a martial-type
job or two for a bit will improve your defenses significantly. There's a lot of
room to customize.

The game fits classic Final Fantasy jobs impressively well into these roles.
These categories don't have names, as far as I can tell, but each job's icon is
color coded. So I'll make up names for them. The first is the classic
all-rounder, including jobs like Knight, Warrior, and Paladin. These jobs do a
bit of everything. Solid offense and defense while offering a small buff for
yourself or the party. Next are the mages, including Black Mage, Red Mage, White
Mage, and so on. They are squishier and don't have many weapon skills, but they
unlock powerful spells. Next are the brutes, a group of jobs including Dragoon,
Samurai, Dark Knight, and so on. They have some ludicrous damage potential, but
play very aggressively, taking lots of risks or directly damaging themselves
while fighting. Finally, the specialists include jobs like Ninja, Thief, and
Assassin. These jobs change the game mechanics in some way, granting the player
great rewards in exchange for specific playstyle restrictions.

To keep each job unique, it has a special skill, unlocked from the start, that
**can't** be transferred to other jobs. This gives you a reason to actually play
each job, rather than treating them as big bags of stats. Even if you _do_ see
them as stats, you'll have to play as them for a while to get those stats, so
you'll experience those unique abilities one way or another---unless you
deliberately avoid it, but who wouldn't get curious?

I very much enjoyed how well they managed to represent each job's playstyle
fantasy while adapting them for this genre. The example everyone talks about is
the Thief, and I will be no different. This is a game without consumable items,
and with plenty of loot already. A Thief job that simply got more loot every
once in a while would be neither fun nor effective. They could have simply left
this job out, but I'm glad they didn't. Instead, they made the Thief steal
_abilities_ from enemies, Blue Mage style. For example, you may steal the
ability to throw rocks from goblins or the ability to deal sonic AoE damage from
bats. These stolen abilities can be quite strong, and generally each encounter
is designed such that stolen abilities will be effective. In particular, stolen
boss abilities tend to be fantastic tools for stagger damage against that boss.

I played this game co-op. Not only is this a nice feature to include, it feels
like this was the intended way to play the game. I'm sure it's very possible and
fun to play solo, but the co-op experience elevated it. When solo, your AI
companions are not particularly good, and they don't interact with the world
very much. On the other hand, with other players, you can plan around each other
in interesting ways, and it's fun to talk about the world around you. There are
many things to find and point out to each other.

My partner immediately gravitated towards the aggressive jobs with daggers and
katanas, while I went for mage jobs. She would rush ahead, start charging up
some crazy anime attack, and I would hang back to support her with healing,
buffs, and heavy artillery. This balance was so fun. Both of us felt like the
other carried us. I think this is a great indication of how cool and different
our ability sets were from each other. We complimented each others' weaknesses
well, and both had flashy moves that were just as entertaining to witness as
they were to activate.

As I said, I played mage jobs primarily, and I think they deserve special
mention here. Their design is so clever. Spells are cast by holding down the
unique job ability button (RT) and picking a spell from a wheel with the left
stick as the circle around the wheel charges up. You can release at any time,
but higher charge levels would cast higher level spells, Fire, Fira, Firaga, and
so on. These spells were not just higher damage, but also had different
projectile and AoE properties. Trying to find opportunities to charge these
spells during hectic combat was already fun enough, but there's another genius
part of this design: you recover mana by hitting things. This is a game that
_wants_ you to be up close and personal, inflicting brutal violence. Mages can't
just hang back, they must be aggressive participants in the fray. Additionally,
you can swift cast the first levels of your spells in the middle of your combos,
once again rewarding you for being as aggressive as possible.

I spent most of my playtime on Sage, a job unlocked after mastering White Mage
and Black Mage. It has the ability to cast all spells from either of those jobs,
in addition to its own unique spell, Ultima. It can't be cast at any time,
however. You must cast three spells on each side of the magic spectrum in order
to prepare it. This reminded me of FFXIV's Black Mage design, one of the most
fun jobs in that game. That's not all! Casting spells repeatedly on one side
causes that side to charge faster and faster until you cast Ultima. At max
charge, the spell charge circle starts at the second level, and progresses to
the third in half the time it usually takes. This makes it easy to spam very
high level spells constantly if you want---or you can sacrifice all that buildup
and power _and a portion of your maximum mana bar_ to cast Ultima. It's
incredibly satisfying every time. Ultima clears rooms. It demolishes boss
phases. I get to shout **UNLEASH ULTIMA.** What's not to love? The job also
exploits elemental weaknesses and throws around buffs like Regen, Haste, and
Shell. The tradeoffs and possibilities are wonderful, extremely fun job design.

But this game has more to it than jobs. There are tons of different weapon
types, each with their own move sets, strengths, weaknesses, and synergies. I
ended up mostly playing with blunt weapons, because it fit: Maces for clerics,
staves for mages, and so on. I quickly found that these weapons are a lot cooler
than they seem. Each staff is not just a stick, but a whip-like chain weapon
that retracts into a staff for spell casting. Katanas can enter a stance that is
immobile but has a far stronger unique move set. With DLC, you can even be a
gunslinger!

The defensive options are also very fun. All weapons can block with varying
levels of effectiveness, and there are parries and other flashy defensive
skills. However, the most fun option is **Soul Shield,** available to all jobs.
When you hold down Soul Shield, your stagger bar rapidly drops, but you'll block
the next attack _and_ regen some mana. Some enemy abilities are also stolen when
blocked like this, though the Thief has additional ways to steal to keep them
unique. Since this ability only blocks the _next_ attack, you'll still sometimes
need to hit a bunch of timed blocks in a row, Sekiro style. Also, there's a wide
window of skill expression here. If you can time the button perfectly, you'll
drain almost none of your resources and only get benefits. However, you can also
lean on the ability with early timing to stay alive, learn the fight/timing, and
play safer. There are also times it is better to rely on other defensive
options, even with perfect skill. It's a very elegant design that allows
everyone to choose their own level of risk and skill expression.

Finally, the finishing blows on staggered enemies are _delightfully_ brutal.
Jack has the ability to partially crystalize others, destroying them from within
and then shattering them. The enemies are all familiar Final Fantasy creatures,
which makes that brutality creative and hilarious every time. Jack will take a
Bomb, crack it over his knee, jam his hands into its mouth, and tear it in half
by ripping the jaws in opposite directions. He will hoist a bipedal creature
across his shoulders, and pull down on its waste and neck until it splits open.
He'll step on a skeleton's back, pull on its skull until it pops off, and then
slam that skull back into the ribcage. He'll grab a harpy by the foot and
overhead slam it back and forth until the leg comes off. Jack is a monster. His
single-mindedness and brutality remind me of Doomguy, transported into Final
Fantasy against his will.

## Everything Else

This is a weird thing to do, but I think this game warrants it. I can't talk
about _any_ other part of this game without getting into spoilers. All of it is
tied not only to the story, but also to a very particular feeling that this game
is trying to give you. That won't work if it is spoiled for you, and this is a
very different kind of spoiler than a twist or a specific story event or
anything like that. I seriously think it is worth playing this game without
reading these spoilers if you are a Final Fantasy fan, especially with a co-op
partner or two. Just know, this game is both very fun and a great tribute to
Final Fantasy. However, here's the lightest possible spoiler:

|| Every level in this game is a reference to a Final Fantasy game. ||

If that at all makes you more interested, stop there and play it. But if you've
already played or you want to read on anyways, I've put everything else behind
this big spoiler toggle:

This game wants you to feel like you are lost in a dreamlike state of déjà vu
where things are not quite right. The set up to this game and the initial
description make this seem like a twisted, dark, absurd remake of the original
Final Fantasy. It starts in the same way. The kingdom of Cornelia sends you, the
Warriors of Light, out to the Chaos Shrine, in search of a knight named Garland.
Sure, Jack is a bit rough and crude for a Warrior of Light, but this is the
right plot, right?

You kill the knight at the Chaos Shrine, who claims to be Chaos, and... out pops
a young girl named Neon. Wait, this isn't right. What's going on here? The
townspeople question the party. These don't seem like Warriors of Light. There
are the wrong number of them. Their crystals are dark. Who are they, and why are
they here? They're not supposed to be here. Something is wrong here.

Well, even if you aren't the Warriors of Light, you're all they've got. You're
sent back out into the world on the prophesied quest, heading to the first town
from the original Final Fantasy, and... instead step into Final Fantasy XIV's
first dungeon, Sastasha. The game doesn't tell you this. It lets you discover
it. I got about halfway through before I realized where I was. This music, these
patches of coral...
[are those pirates? Probably!](https://ffxiv.consolegameswiki.com/wiki/It%27s_Probably_Pirates)

Weirder still, everything is a bit out of place. The music starts out [haunting
and disorienting,](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=9zHam6CbSzo), like a dream
of a song you've heard before, half remembered. Then it becomes more clear,
resembling
[the original track from FFXIV,](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=QNgcCnGR_3E)
but [some of the instruments are swapped around and it still sounds wrong
somehow.](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=I8j04e19rBU) It never quite sounds
right. The world design is eerily familiar, but it doesn't quite match Sastasha.
The tunnels are more confusing and labyrinthine. The colors are exaggerated.

Only moments after the connection is made in your mind and you start to notice
the differences, Final Fantasy XIV fades away. You don't fight a Sahagin at the
end of this dungeon. You fight Bikke, the pirate captain from Final Fantasy. As
expected, he apologizes and sends you on your way to the next part of that
game's plot. It's like that whole FFXIV thing never happened. Gaslighting
through game design.

Every area is like this. The more Final Fantasy games you've played, the more
uncanny and familiar everything will feel. Some of them are far more subtle than
others, and very rarely is the chosen area one of the most iconic areas from
that game. The Final Fantasy VII location is the underwater reactor. That one
took me _forever_ to figure out, but it was on the tip of my tongue the whole
time. The reason I was trying so hard to figure it out was because I knew it had
to be _something_ thanks to the more obvious ones, such as the FFVI location:
the Floating Continent, complete with Warring Triad and all. The oscillation
between subtle and obvious kept me guessing and put me in a state of mind where
I was always suspicious and confused. It was fantastic.

The reason this is brilliant is **this is happening to your characters, too.**
They are in a confused and suspicious state, because their memories were taken
from them. They keep thinking they recognize things, before shaking the feeling
off and pretending it never happened. But there's this growing sense that
_something is wrong here._

As you push on, the original story of Final Fantasy continues to unravel. You
uncover a fifth warrior of light? That's not right. Is someone mistaken here? Is
there a trick being played? Potentially more strangely, both Neon and Sophia
emerged from monsters you defeated. Oh well. They say they're here to kill
Chaos. That's a good enough reason to join the party.

I'll skip over the bulk of the story, because recapping everything would be
lame, but I'll emphasize that the mysteries continue to get deeper and the
unsettled feelings continue to grow. This culminates in Jack being revealed to
be Jack _Garland_, and if you know your Final Fantasy lore, you know he's the
one who becomes Chaos. The party attacks him, not because they hate him, but
because they know the pain of losing them and the confusion of the sudden
betrayal is the only way to awaken Jack to emotions strong enough to turn him
into Chaos. This is a brutal fight, against the party you've spent time building
and getting to know. Jack kills each one in his signature ultra-violent style,
because it's the only thing he knows how to do. In anguish, he cries out and
absorbs the world's darkness. This puts you in a permanent version of a
super-state you've had unlocked since the beginning of the game, and sends you
off on a rampage to defeat the true villains. It's time to kill god.

In so doing, Jack resets the timeline on his own terms, not the terms of those
who previously manipulated this world. That party of five? It turns out they
were Chaos plus the Four Fiends from the original game, not the four Warriors of
Light at all. Jack and his friends become the villains, secretly saving the
world from a worse fate and daring it to cultivate heroes strong enough to
finally truly defeat them and therefore prove themselves strong enough to be the
prophesied Warriors of Light. Until then, they'll reset the world over and over.
This is their eternal vigil.

This is a prequel to Final Fantasy. It is also a tribute to it, the entire
series, and the spirit that ties all the games together.

### Music

I had to put this section behind spoilers, because so many tracks either
directly reference motifs from earlier entries in the series or do that
indirect, dreamlike kind of reference I talked about above. That would spoil the
surprise, and I think these songs have the most impact when experienced for the
first time in context. However, you're here, reading this, so I have permission
to spoil away.

This soundtrack is massive. It's larger than FFXV's. They put more songs into
this game than any other mainline Final Fantasy game up to this point. It makes
sense---they had a lot of games to reference, and each of those needed several
entries to achieve the intended effect. To achieve this, they brought in a team
composers: Naoshi Mizuta leading, with Ryo Yamazaki, Hidenori Iwasaki, and
Yoshitaka Suzuki in supporting roles. I'll keep this short, though, and only
reference a few favorites.

I will start with Ryo Yamazaki. He has worked on a number of Square Enix titles
over the years, but it appears that this was by far one of his biggest roles. He
brought a lot of variety to the soundtrack, from melancholic, dreamlike
renditions of tracks from other games like
[Refrin](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=tzrqgaCn0z8) from FFXIII to
explosive, energetic tracks like
[Shadows Rising](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=7YO8MlRMBfE) (one of the most
popular tracks on the OST).

Hidenori Iwasaki is in a similar camp, having worked on numerous Square Enix
titles but rarely one so high profile. My favorite tracks of his are
[Battle: Dragon Zombie](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=lPfb5Yyj5jw) and
[Battle: Darkness](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=Q4BM9cn_osE). This heavy,
electronic, industrial style was a very fun addition to the soundtrack and
helped sell some terrifying threats.

One of my favorite tracks in the entire game,
[Battle: The Fiend of Fire](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=Q4BM9cn_osE) was
composed by Yoshitaka Suzuki. Absolute banger. If you listen to a single song I
link to, let it be this. This is no surprise---Suzuki has composed for Metal
Gear Solid 4, Bayonetta, Kingdom Hearts, Lightning Returns, Final Fantasy XIV,
Final Fantasy XV, and the Final Fantasy VII Remake series. He's everywhere, and
for good reason. Another of his fantastic entries to this OST is
[Battle: The Fiend of Wind](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=exG6ddkvBUE). I
love how these two tracks pull motifs from the earliest music in Final Fantasy
history, and reimagine them in this pulsing, driving style with so much going
on. I enjoy how they kept these Fiend themes consistent in style.

And of course, I can't forget Naoshi Mizuta, who is quickly becoming another of
my favorite Final Fantasy composers. The first music of his that I heard was
from the Final Fantasy XIII trilogy, and his contributions to those soundtracks
are a large part of why they are my favorites in the series. I hear a lot of the
FFXIII style in his tracks for this game, and that makes me appreciate both this
soundtrack and those soundtracks even more. He took the lead composer role for
this project, so the bulk of the tracks are his. The track heard most commonly
is [Battle: The Warrior](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=CPQx0Mo6t9w), because
it is the main combat theme. It's such a banger and so easy to listen to on
repeat. I'll also highlight
[Jack's Theme](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=nhaQPvhgysU&si=r9H) because it
_goes places_ and it's such a fitting main theme for the game.
[Battle: Chaos Advent](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=pm_s64XaLu8) also
drives me crazy in the best way. Such a Lightning Returns beat.
[Battle: Become Chaos](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=b2aMjAr-uPQ) is
fittingly chaotic and fun. It's crazy for that to back the final phase of the
final fight in the game. It subverts so many conventions for that style of track
while still giving them nods in interesting ways. Finally, he strikes the heart
directly with emotional tracks like
[Solitude](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=tbIFJ7QapRg). If I keep going, I
will list every one of his tracks, because they're all perfect.

This is a fantastic soundtrack. The variety and quality remind me a lot of the
FFXIII-2 soundtrack, which I also enjoy quite a bit, but I think I place this
one higher. It's not my favorite in the series, but it's damn close. It's
_unreasonably good_ for a spinoff soundtrack, and I love it even more for that.
These songs will be in my rotation for a long time.

# Final Thoughts

**Rating: 8/10** \
_Playtime: 14 hours_

This game is an absolute treat, amplified by the player's love for the
franchise. It has my favorite approach to referencing other games in the series.
The tributes are well done and so clever, without feeling like marketing
gimmicks or theme park attractions. It's respectful and clearly was made with an
incredible amount of passion.

I have very few complaints. The only reasons it doesn't score higher are that
its lower budget is visible in quite a few places, the core character designs
are nothing special, and the main story is focused on those respectful and
clever references rather than having super emotionally impactful themes. There
are also some Nioh-isms that just don't work for me very well, but those are
more neutral points than negative. Overall, these are things it lacked that
would push it to the top for me, not things I hold against it.

Those flaws almost make me want to like this game a little more. They give it a
sort of underdog feeling. I love what this game does well, and I love that it
exists. So far, this is **definitely** the best spinoff in the series, and
pretty handily a top ten entry overall. We need more small scale, mid-budget
games like this from this franchise. The heavily experimental nature of this
game reassures me that the wild and creative spirit of Final Fantasy is still
alive out there, and it showed itself through one of the best works of self-love
the series has yet seen.
