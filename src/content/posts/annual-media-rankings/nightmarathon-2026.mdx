I keep saving horror games for October. Then October arrives with too many games
to play. I should know by now that mental notes mean nothing to me. So I'm
starting a tradition: Nightmarathon is my annual marathon of horror games. This
will likely be mostly short, focused experiences, but I'll throw in some larger
games as applicable for my time and interest levels.

My plan is to post this at the start of the month and update it over time as I
play games. No need to wait till the end.

## Ena: Dream BBQ
**Rating: 7/10**  
_Playtime: 1 hour_

**WHERE THE HELL IS THE BOSS**

I knew going into this game that it was a short, weird little experience. I
didn't realize that it's essentially the free demo for an episodic series that
hasn't started releasing yet. So I'm left a bit disappointed by the lack of any
kind of real ending. This is hardly even any exposition! It's also apparently
part of an ongoing series of animated videos, not stand-alone.

Still, it is absurdly creative and creatively absurd. This game jumps between
visual styles and narrative tropes like it is trying to speedrun through every
nightmare anyone has had after falling asleep with cartoons on. It is the
wasteland of the collective unconscious. It is surreal in a way only a human
mind could create.

What I find most impressive about it are all the wild animations. There are tons
of them, many custom for particular interactions only seen once, and most are
hand drawn. The ones that aren't are in some other style for effect, like how
the witches in Madoka Magica stand out. It goes deeper however, because there
are a ridiculous number of _different styles_ of hand-drawn animation, too.

Daryl talks about it in [this video](https://youtu.be/URNOsmqJEng ) (at the time
of writing, his latest). That's where I first heard of this game, and I was
intrigued enough to go play it myself. I think Daryl does a better job than I
could possibly do here, even in a video technically about something else.

I don't know if I necessarily recommend this one until more content is out. But
it is free and it is short. Might be worth experiencing!

## Iron Lung
**Rating: 8/10**  
_Playtime: 1 hour_

This is the distilled essence of the fear of the unknown, with sides of
claustrophobia and confusion and a ticking time bomb of tension. The scope is
kept extremely precise, and that adds to the feeling of smallness the game
evokes.

Iron Lung puts you in a tiny submarine with no windows. All you have is a map, a
live reading of your current coordinates, and blinking lights warning you when
you get close to things. There's one single camera facing forward, but you can't
see it from the submarine's controls. You must walk to the back of the vessel to
hit the button. You can't drive and take pictures at the same time. Pictures
come out grainy, monochrome, and have a tiny field of view. This is your only
way to view the outside world.

Everything is intentionally confusing. You've given a database computer at the
back of the ship. I never figured out how to make it do anything. I think that
was the point. You're given vague details about what to do because this is not a
mission you are meant to return from. This is an execution in all but name.
You're taught that very quickly. I died several times just trying to figure out
the controls. I was being told that Iron Lung won't wait for me. It won't give
me an inch. This is a deadly game and I don't know the rules.

That unknown element is important. You don't know what's outside your vessel.
You don't know how to control this vessel. You've been put in a strange, clumsy
body and forced to walk through a minefield. If there are terrors at the bottom
of our own ocean, what lies beneath an ocean of blood on an alien world? Even
your purpose down here is to see things that have never been seen before.

That's where some of the genius comes in. The game is made up of very few parts,
but all of them are used to their maximum potential to cause horror. I won't
spoil the details further.

This is a great, self contained experience and I highly recommend it.

</GameEntry>

## Moldrise
**Rating: 8/10**  
_Playtime: 1 hour (varies)_

_**Why are you reading this? You must go up!**_

Moldrise is structured sort of like an adventure game. You need to get the fuse
from the janitor but he lost his flashlight and the guy upstairs will give you
one but only in trade for a bag of KRAZY Krunchy Nuts™ which you need to buy
with coin which you can get by selling junk to the lady on the first floor but
only after you deliver her newspaper for her and---*deep breath.* That's just
the first step. Meanwhile, every step you take makes you hungrier, thirstier,
and _you need to go up._ Every time a quest tells you to turn around and go back
for something, it's another threat on your life.

You're going to die. That's just part of it. Fortunately you can use your
knowledge from previous playthroughs to take advantage of shortcuts. Maybe on
your second playthrough you'll be able to skip the whole flashlight thing,
saving tons of time---and more importantly, resources. The game even has a
mechanic for this, automatically documenting the most important bits of
knowledge for you and saving them between runs. All just a button push away.
This makes it feel like each run is not an individual life, but a part of a
bigger collective. **Like a colony of fungus.**

The vibes are the most impressive thing about this game. It leverages its space
and your repetitive journey through it in extremely clever ways. It reminds me
of Mouthwashing in that way. It's more of a disturbing game than a horror game.
Everything is surreal and uncanny. The more the mold progresses, your perception
of the world becomes more twisted and _more about going up **(you must).**_

I highly recommend this game, but it won't appeal to everyone. Hopefully I've
given you enough detail to tell if it intrigues you or not.

</GameEntry>

## MOLE
**Rating: 9/10**  
_Playtime: 3 hours_

The opposite of Moldrise, MOLE isolates you in a small space and asks you to go
down. It feels in some ways like a more fleshed out version of Iron Lung, fused
with 2024's hit horror game, Mouthwashing.

You are on the Mole, a drilling vessel, with a mission to find the White Rabbit
Signal which has been causing supernatural disasters and driving people mad.

You are the vessel's pilot and navigator. Most of the gameplay revolves around
completing the mission, and the game makes that very tactile. You don't just
open that locked door once you have the key. You must put the key in the lock,
spin it manually, and _then_ open the door. You operate machinery by reading
instructions and pulling the right levers, sliding the pieces into place,
manually opening little doors, and so on. There are no parts of any process that
are automated for you, unless an in-universe computer is doing it. The game is
very clever with how this is all implemented. It gets you to learn these
processes early and makes them feel routine. Then it puts the pressure on. Will
you fumble the key when death approaches? Will you remember to hold down that
button for the right amount of time when every second brings you closer to
death? This is a perfectly tuned engine to produce as much tension as possible.

The game's aesthetic is a mashup of overlapping, anachronistic styles. It is
designed to look somewhat like a PS1 game, in 3D but with heavily pixelated
textures. Yet, there's far more detail and quality in here than the PS1 could
possibly handle, and some objects are more detailed than others to make them
stand out. The big, boxy terminals run on cartridges that are somehow both
computer chips and cassette tapes. There are digital displays overlaid with
analogue meters. Your scientific equipment looks like it was thrown together in
a garage by a lunatic. It's a wonderful mess and extremely well prepared for
your imagination.

This had a near-perfect balance of horror for me. There are tons of slow-burn
psychological elements, but it's not afraid to throw you into a panicked
scramble. There are jumpscares, but they always feel earned and are not over
relied on. There are oppressive atmospheres sometimes, while other times leaving
the door open with your back turned is enough to be unsettling. I found myself
optimizing my tasks around making sure I could see the entrances and exits as
much as possible. I think that's very intentional. For example, the game lets
you pick up a phone and move around the room with it (within cord range).
There's no gameplay reason to allow this other than to let the player be
paranoid. So many details fit that pattern. No paranoia or fear opportunity is
missed.

This game has set a pretty high bar for the rest of the month already. There are
plenty of games to go, but I doubt any are going to be quite this good. If they
are, I will be very happily surprised. This will place well on my GotY ranking.
MOLE gets my highest recommendation so far. For $10 (currently on sale) and 3
hours of your time, it's like going to a movie.
